# Bestiary lore — Oozes & Slimes, Vermin & Cave Crawlers, Plants & Fungi, Aquatic & the Drowned Deep

Lore for the first four bestiary families of Abysmal. This document is the lore home: it tells where each creature comes from, what stories it carries, and what it looks like. It contains no game rules. Where a name is generic or game-original and no real tradition stands behind it, the entry says so plainly rather than inventing one.

## Oozes & Slimes

The ooze is one of fantasy's youngest monsters and one of its most honest: a creature with no face, no plan, and no mercy, only appetite and patience. It descends less from ancient myth than from the amoeba under a microscope, the slime mould on a rotting log, and the cave drip that never quite stops. Tabletop games gave it a dungeon to live in, films gave it a hunger that grows with every meal, and from there it spread into almost every fantasy world that has dark, wet places in it.

### Slime

The slime is the archetype of the whole family. Its ancestors are not gods or demons but single-celled life: the amoeba that flows around its food and swallows it whole, the slime mould that creeps across damp wood as a single pulsing mass, and the cave formations known as snottites, colonies of bacteria that hang from cave ceilings and drip acid. Early tabletop fantasy took that biology, enlarged it, and set it loose in dungeons, where a slow, nearly transparent mass that digests whatever it touches became a perfect low-level terror: too mindless to bargain with, too shapeless to stab in any one place, and easy to walk into if you are not watching the floor.

From there the slime multiplied into a whole bestiary of its own. The gelatinous cube, a transparent block exactly fitted to a dungeon corridor, became a joke and a classic at once. Film gave the world the Blob, a growing, engulfing mass from the sky. Video games made the slime cute as often as they made it deadly, turning it into a mascot, a pet, and a first enemy all at once. That double life is the point: a slime is never impressive, which is exactly why it is dangerous. It does not need to be.

- **Defining traits:** shapeless, boneless, and patient; flows around obstacles rather than climbing over them; engulfs rather than bites.
- **Variants:** clear jellies, green dungeon slimes, and the famous corridor-filling cube are all faces of the same idea.

### Cave Jelly

**Thin lore — director call.**

The Cave Jelly has no story of its own in folklore or older fantasy. It is a game-original variation on the slime idea, distinguished by where it lives and how it hunts: the cave ceiling and wall rather than the open floor. Its nearest real-world cousins are the dripping, gelatinous growths found in real caves — bacterial colonies and slime moulds that hang, glisten, and slowly spread over stone — and the old folk notion of "star jelly," the strange translucent masses sometimes found in fields after rain, which people once took for fallen stars or the leavings of meteors.

What the Cave Jelly does carry, honestly, is the cave itself as a tradition. Caves in folklore are places where things drip, grow, and wait in the dark, and where looking up is as important as looking ahead. A jelly that uses the ceiling as its hunting ground belongs to that tradition of cave dread, even if no single tale names it.

- **Defining traits:** translucent, glistening, and at home on ceilings and walls; drops rather than charges.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; colour and glow are the usual ways one cave jelly is told from another.

### Amber Ooze

**Thin lore — director call.**

Amber is fossilised tree resin, and it has carried stories for far longer than any ooze has. Ancient amber washed up on Baltic shores was traded across Europe, carved into beads and amulets, and said to hold sunlight, lightning, or the tears of gods and mourners — in Greek telling, the sisters of Phaethon wept amber after his fall. Insects and leaves trapped inside it made it a natural symbol of things preserved, stuck, and held fast forever.

The Amber Ooze takes that one quality — stickiness that does not let go — and gives it a body. There is no amber monster in folklore; there is only amber itself, golden, warm-looking, and treacherous to anything small enough to land in it while it is still soft. An ooze made of hardened resin is therefore best read as amber's warning made animate: beautiful, golden, and impossible to pull free from once it has you.

- **Defining traits:** golden and resinous, hardening as it sets; clings and holds rather than dissolving.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; paler honey tones and darker, older resin are natural variations on the theme.

### Mire Pudding

**Thin lore — director call.**

The Mire Pudding belongs to the bog, and bogs have a folklore of their own even if this particular creature does not. Across Britain, Ireland, and northern Europe, bogs were places that preserved what fell into them — bodies, butter, weapons, whole wooden trackways — and places that swallowed travellers who misread solid ground. Will-o'-the-wisps danced over them, and stories warned that the ground itself could be hungry. A pudding of stagnant sediment is that warning with a shape: not a spirit or a god, but the mire itself, half-liquid, half-solid, and impossible to tell from the ground around it until it moves.

Its real-world relatives are mud itself and the things that live in it: the sucking bog, the stagnant pool that looks shallow and is not, and the grey, churning sediment of a marsh at low water. The name "pudding" is a piece of dungeon humour with a long pedigree — fantasy has always named its oozes after food, from jellies to puddings — and it suits a creature that is, in essence, the floor deciding to eat you.

- **Defining traits:** murky, half-sunk, and easily mistaken for ordinary mud or shallow water.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; bog, marsh, and stagnant-pool forms differ only in colour and smell.

### Acid Blob

The Acid Blob is the ooze at its most frankly chemical. Fantasy's acid oozes descend from the black pudding and the ochre jelly of early tabletop games, blobs whose touch dissolved armour, flesh, and anything else organic, and whose only real defence was that they were slow and mindless. Behind those game monsters stand two real inspirations: the corrosive drips of cave bacteria, which excrete sulphuric acid as part of how they live, and the digestive chemistry of real life — stomach acid, venom, and the enzymes that break a meal down outside the body before it is absorbed.

Acid also has a folk life of its own. Alchemists dreamed of universal solvents, waters that could dissolve anything; miners feared the acidic waters that ate tools and skin in deep workings; and every culture with vinegar, vitriol, or lye knew a liquid that could eat through what it touched. The Acid Blob is that knowledge made hungry: a creature that does not need teeth because its whole body is a solvent, and whose churn and bubbling are the visible sign of digestion already under way.

- **Defining traits:** restless and churning, its surface bubbling before it strikes; dissolves rather than tears.
- **Variants:** tabletop tradition splits acid oozes by colour and temper — black, ochre, and grey forms are the best-known cousins.

### Gloom Ooze

**Thin lore — director call.**

There is no gloom ooze in folklore, but there is a very old fear behind it: darkness that is not empty. Folk tales are full of shadows that move wrong, of corners of a room that seem darker than they should be, and of caves and cellars where the dark feels thick, as if it could be touched. The Gloom Ooze gives that feeling a body — sludge the colour of a lightless place, dimming what little light comes near it, and folding over its prey the way night folds over a road.

Its closest real cousins are not monsters at all but phenomena: the black, light-swallowing water of a deep pool, the ink a cuttlefish throws to blind and escape, and the way a cave's darkness seems to press inward once the lamp goes out. As a monster it is therefore a mood before it is a creature, and that is honestly all the lore it has. Anything more would be invented.

- **Defining traits:** umbral and light-dimming, favouring shadow over open ground; smothers as much by darkness as by weight.
- **Variants:** none in tradition.

### Mercury Slime

**Thin lore — director call.**

Mercury, the liquid metal the Romans named quicksilver, has one of the richest and strangest histories of any substance in folklore and early science. Alchemists made it central to their art: it was fluid yet metallic, poisonous yet beautiful, and they believed it held the secret of transformation, able to dissolve gold and carry the properties of other metals. It was also a medicine, a mirror backing, a hat-maker's poison, and a thing of terrible beauty — a silver liquid that beads, scatters, and reforms, and that no hand can hold.

The Mercury Slime borrows all of that at once. No myth tells of a living quicksilver creature, but the metal's own behaviour — pooling, splitting into shining droplets, flowing together again, reflecting everything around it — is already halfway to a monster. A slime of mercury is heavy, bright, and treacherous in the same way the metal is: lovely to look at, quick to scatter, and dangerous to touch. Its lore is therefore mercury's lore, transferred to a body that moves.

- **Defining traits:** heavy, metallic, and mirror-bright; scatters into droplets and reforms; quick for an ooze.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; silver is the defining colour, with darker, tarnished forms as a natural variation.

### Primordial Ooze

**Thin lore — director call.**

The Primordial Ooze is the family's creation myth, and creation myths are where it gets its only real ancestry. Many traditions begin the world not with a god but with a sea, a mud, or a formless deep: the waters of Nun in Egyptian telling, the chaos before order in Greek telling, the mud from which life crawls in countless folk cosmologies. Modern science tells a parallel story in the "primordial soup," the warm, chemical-rich waters from which the first living things are thought to have assembled. A raw protoplasm, older than any shaped creature, is that beginning still moving — life before it learned to have a form, a face, or a limit.

As a monster, the Primordial Ooze is therefore less a species than a remainder: what was there before everything else, and what might still be underneath it all. It has no tales of its own because it predates tales. Its horror is scale and age rather than malice. It does not hate its prey; it simply absorbs it, the way the first sea absorbed everything that fell into it.

- **Defining traits:** ancient, slow, and tide-like; reshapes itself around obstacles; engulfs on a grand scale.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; it is, by definition, the form before forms diverged.

## Vermin & Cave Crawlers

Vermin are the dungeon's working class: small, numerous, and everywhere that larger monsters cannot be bothered to look. Their lore is a mixture of real natural history — rats in granaries, bats in belfries, spiders in corners — and the folk dread that grew up around them, in which every small creeping thing might be a plague carrier, a witch's familiar, or a spy for something worse. Caves made that dread literal. A cave is a closed world with its own rules, and the things that thrive in it are the things that do not need light, space, or permission.

### Cave Rat

The rat is one of humanity's oldest neighbours and oldest enemies. Rats followed grain stores, ships, and armies across the world, and folklore followed them: rats as plague bearers, rats as thieves in the night, rats as the shape a witch or a devil might take, and rats as an omen when they desert a house or a ship. The Pied Piper of Hamelin is the most famous rat story in Europe, but the everyday version is older and simpler — the gnawing heard in the walls at night, the food store found spoiled in the morning, the sense that something small and clever is living just out of sight and knows your house better than you do.

The cave rat is that neighbour moved underground, where it no longer needs to hide from people at all. Real cave rats and their kin — pack rats, wood rats, and the rodents that colonise mines and cellars — hoard, gnaw, and breed in the dark, and they learn the tunnels the way a city rat learns alleys. A cave rat is therefore not exotic; it is familiar, which is why it unnerves. It is the animal that was already living in your walls, now living in the walls of the world below.

- **Defining traits:** quick, darting, and food-driven; nips from the side rather than meeting a foe head-on; flees light and sudden noise unless cornered.
- **Variants:** sewer rats, ship rats, and pack rats are regional faces of the same animal; cave forms are typically paler and bolder in the dark.

### Rat Swarm

A single rat is a nuisance. A swarm is a disaster, and folklore has always known the difference. Medieval chronicles told of rat plagues that stripped fields and storehouses; later tales gave the swarm a king — the Rat King, a knot of rats tangled together by their tails, said to appear as an omen of famine or plague — and modern horror gave it a tide, a living carpet that flows over everything in its path. The swarm works as a monster precisely because it has no leader to kill and no single body to wound. You cannot duel a swarm. You can only get out of its way, or be eaten by it in small bites.

Real rat behaviour feeds the legend. Rats are social, they follow scent trails laid by their fellows, and a colony moving together can look, from ground level, like a single creature with a hundred teeth. The swarm in story is that colony seen by someone who is already afraid: not a hundred animals but one hunger, wearing a hundred bodies.

- **Defining traits:** flows as one mass, splits around obstacles, and rejoins; overwhelms by numbers and teeth rather than strength.
- **Variants:** the Rat King — rats knotted tail-to-tail — is the swarm's most famous folk variant, more omen than army.

### Cave Bat

Bats have lived in human folklore far longer than they have lived in caves in stories. To many cultures they were creatures between categories — neither bird nor beast, active when honest animals slept — and that in-betweenness made them suspects: companions of witches, messengers of the underworld, or souls that had not settled. In Maya tradition the bat god Camazotz, the "death bat," was a creature of the night and of sacrifice. In European telling, bats in a belfry or a bedroom were ill omens, and a bat flying into a house might mean a death in the family. At the same time, Chinese tradition read the bat very differently: the word for bat sounds like the word for good fortune, and bats became emblems of luck and long life.

The cave bat strips away the symbolism and keeps the animal: a small, winged mammal that navigates by sound, roosts in huge colonies in the dark, and hunts insects on the wing with turns no bird can match. Its erratic flight, which looks like madness to a watcher's eye, is precision to the bat — it is flying by echo, in a world made of sound rather than sight. In a cave, that makes it the perfect native: it owns the dark in a way no visitor ever will.

- **Defining traits:** erratic, swooping flight; navigates by sound; roosts in colonies and hunts on the wing.
- **Variants:** fruit bats, insect-eaters, and the great cave colonies of the tropics differ in size and diet, not in their mastery of the dark.

### Cave Spider

Spiders sit at the crossroads of folklore: weavers, trappers, and patient killers. The Greek tale of Arachne, the mortal weaver who challenged Athena and was turned into a spider for her pride, made the spider the emblem of craft punished by arrogance. West African and Caribbean tales made Anansi the spider a trickster hero, cleverer than lions and gods. Across Europe, spiders in the house were luck or warning depending on the telling, and a spider dropping onto a person could mean money, a letter, or a death. Every version agrees on one thing: the spider does not chase. It waits, and the waiting is the weapon.

The cave spider is that patience moved into a place where waiting works even better. Real cave spiders — the cellar spiders, orb-weavers of mine shafts, and the pale, long-legged hunters of limestone caves — build in corners, ceilings, and disused passages, and feel their prey through vibration long before they see it. A web in a cave is not decoration; it is a sense organ stretched across a corridor. The cave spider's lore is therefore the lore of the trap itself: the corner you did not check, the thread you did not see, and the stillness that was never empty.

- **Defining traits:** motionless ambusher; senses prey through vibration in its web; strikes only when prey is committed.
- **Variants:** orb-weavers, cellar spiders, and funnel-web builders are all cave-capable forms; cave specimens tend toward paleness and longer legs.

### Cave Wasp

Wasps have never enjoyed the bee's good reputation. Where bees make honey and keep a hive, wasps in folklore are thieves, tormentors, and soldiers: creatures that sting without dying for it, that rob other insects' nests, and that gather in angry clouds around a disturbed nest. Old sayings warned against poking a wasp's nest for the same reason they warned against poking any armed camp. At the same time, wasps were respected as hunters in their own right — solitary wasps that paralyse spiders and caterpillars to feed their young are among the most precise predators in the insect world, and their sting is a tool, not a temper.

The cave wasp carries that reputation underground. Real cave wasps are rarer than cave bees, but wasps of many kinds nest in sheltered, dark places — hollow trees, burrows, rock crevices, and the mouths of caves — and defend those nests with a ferocity out of proportion to their size. A wasp in a cave is therefore a small creature guarding a small territory with total commitment, and its sting is the cave's way of saying that not every small thing in the dark is prey.

- **Defining traits:** hovering, circling flight; defends a nest territory fiercely; stings and withdraws rather than grappling.
- **Variants:** paper wasps, solitary hunting wasps, and yellowjackets differ in nest and temper; all share the sting-and-retreat pattern.

### Cave Scorpion

The scorpion is one of the oldest animals in folklore, and one of the most consistently feared. In Egyptian telling it belonged to Serqet, the goddess who protected against venomous stings even as she embodied them. In Greek telling, the scorpion that killed Orion was set among the stars as Scorpio, still chasing the hunter across the sky. In desert cultures generally, the scorpion was the small, armoured thing that could kill a king with a single sting — a reminder that size and danger have nothing to do with each other. Its tail, carried high and curled, became a universal sign: approach carefully, or not at all.

Real scorpions are ancient in a literal sense, too, little changed across hundreds of millions of years, and cave scorpions are among their most specialised forms: pale, long-limbed, and sensitive to the faintest vibration in stone. A cave scorpion does not need to see its prey. It feels it coming through the ground, waits with claws open, and lets the tail do the talking. Its lore is therefore a lore of patience and of armour — a creature that has survived every age by being hard, still, and very sure of its sting.

- **Defining traits:** slow, deliberate advance; claws open, tail carried high and curled; strikes with pincer and tail together.
- **Variants:** desert, forest, and cave scorpions differ in size, colour, and venom; cave forms are paler and more vibration-sensitive.

### Centipede

Centipedes appear in folklore less as monsters than as warnings: the many-legged thing under the stone, the fast ripple in the corner of the eye, the bite that burns out of proportion to the animal's size. In tropical traditions, where giant centipedes are real and their bites genuinely dangerous, they earned a more serious reputation — household guardians in some tellings, venomous intruders in others, and in parts of Asia a creature whose appearance in a home could be read as either luck or threat. The common thread is speed and surprise. A centipede does not loom. It is suddenly there, moving in a wave of legs, and then it is somewhere else.

Real centipedes are among the fastest ground predators of their size, and their hunting method is as distinctive as their shape: they seize prey with modified front legs that work like venomous fangs, then wrap and hold it while the venom works. Cave centipedes take that method into the dark, hunting along walls and floor seams where their many legs give them grip no single pair of feet could match. Their lore is the lore of the floor itself turning hostile — the ground that suddenly has legs.

- **Defining traits:** rippling, many-legged crawl; hugs walls and seams; seizes with venomous forelegs and curls around prey.
- **Variants:** house centipedes, tropical giants, and pale cave forms differ greatly in size; all share the wave-like gait.

### Cave Frog

Frogs are transformation animals in folklore almost everywhere they appear. In European tales a frog may be a prince under enchantment, a witch's familiar, or the creature whose croak warns of rain. In Egyptian telling the frog goddess Heqet attended births and embodied fertility and renewal. In Chinese tradition the three-legged money frog brought wealth. And in the rain-forest traditions of Central and South America, the brightly coloured poison frogs were living warnings: beauty that should not be touched, colour as a sign that says, plainly, do not eat me.

The cave frog is the humbler cousin of all those stories: the frog that lives where the water gathers underground, in pools, drips, and flooded passages. Real cave frogs and cave-dwelling amphibians are often pale, large-eyed, and sensitive to vibration and movement, and their leap — sudden, long, and hard to predict — is their main defence and their main weapon. A frog in a cave is easy to dismiss until it moves, and that is the whole of its folk character: unremarkable, damp, and suddenly airborne.

- **Defining traits:** hopping advance with a crouched pause between leaps; sensitive to small movements; strikes with tongue or a bodily leap.
- **Variants:** pool frogs, tree frogs, and poison frogs are surface cousins; cave forms are paler, larger-eyed, and more vibration-aware.

### Giant Rat

The giant rat is folklore's escalation of a real fear: that the small thing in the walls might not stay small. Tales of rats as large as dogs or pigs appear in city legends, in wartime stories, and in horror fiction, where the giant rat is the vermin problem grown past all control — the sewer, the plague pit, or the abandoned district producing a rat that no longer flees from people. Real giant rats exist, too, though they are less monstrous than the stories: the pouched rats of Africa, the giant rats of New Guinea and the Philippines, and the capybara's distant rodent kin all reach sizes that surprise anyone who thinks "rat" means "small."

As a dungeon creature, the giant rat is the cave rat with its caution burned away. Where the small rat darts and flees, the giant rat charges, rears, and mauls, because it no longer needs to be afraid of what it meets in a tunnel. Its lore is therefore a lore of scale: take a familiar pest, remove its fear, and watch what happens to the balance of a place.

- **Defining traits:** bold, heavy skirmisher; charges shoulder-first rather than darting; mauls at close range.
- **Variants:** African pouched rats, island giant rats, and sewer legends are real and folk cousins; all share the loss of small-rat timidity.

### Vampire Bat

The vampire bat is a rare case of a real animal named after a monster, rather than the other way round. European vampire folklore — the restless dead who rise to drink the blood of the living — was centuries old before explorers in Central and South America found bats that actually fed on blood. The bats were named for the monsters, and then, through Bram Stoker's Dracula and a century of film, the monsters were given the bats' wings in return. In Maya tradition, the bat had its own older and darker life: Camazotz, the death bat of the Popol Vuh, was a god of night and sacrifice, and bats in Maya art were creatures of the underworld as much as of the air.

The real vampire bat is stranger and gentler than the legend, and more interesting for it. Only three species feed on blood, and they do not suck it: they make a small, nearly painless cut with razor teeth and lap the blood as it flows, helped by an anticoagulant in their saliva. They feed mostly on livestock and birds, rarely on people, and they share food with hungry roost-mates, regurgitating blood for bats that failed to feed — a social kindness in the service of a parasitic diet. The vampire bat's lore is therefore two stories braided together: the folk vampire's dread, and the real bat's quiet, communal, blood-only life.

- **Defining traits:** silent, gliding flight; feeds by lapping blood from a small cut, not by draining; drawn to warmth, breath, and sleeping prey.
- **Variants:** the common vampire bat, the hairy-legged vampire bat, and the white-winged vampire bat are the three blood-feeding species, all of the Americas.

### Widow Spider

The widow spider takes its name from a story that is only partly true, and the exaggeration is the point of its folklore. Female widow spiders sometimes eat the male after mating, and that single fact — a bride who devours her bridegroom — was enough for English-speaking North America to name the whole genus for it. The glossy black body and the red hourglass on the underside of the abdomen did the rest: a warning mark, bright as a signal, on a creature that otherwise hides in dark, undisturbed corners. In popular telling the widow became a symbol of hidden danger, of female power, and of patience that ends in a single, decisive bite.

The science is calmer and still impressive. Widows belong to the genus Latrodectus, whose name means roughly "secret biter," and their venom is a potent neurotoxin — among the most medically significant of any North American spider — though bites are rarely fatal to healthy adults and the spider itself is shy, biting only when pressed against skin. Widows build tangled, irregular webs rather than neat orbs, and they hang upside-down in them, waiting. A widow spider's lore is therefore the lore of the corner you do not disturb: the dark place, the tangled web, and the small red mark that says you were warned.

- **Defining traits:** hangs motionless in a dense, tangled web; glossy black with a red hourglass marking; bites only when prey or a hand is committed.
- **Variants:** southern, northern, and western black widows, and the Australian redback, are regional cousins with the same warning colours.

### Emperor Scorpion

The emperor scorpion is real before it is legendary, and its reality is grand enough to need little embellishment. Pandinus imperator, native to the rainforests of West Africa, is one of the largest scorpions in the world, dark and heavily armoured, with massive pincers it uses more for crushing and holding than for stinging. Unlike the desert scorpions of folklore, whose sting is their whole reputation, the emperor is a grappler first: it seizes, holds, and crushes, and its sting, while present, is comparatively mild. Keepers and traders made it famous as a display animal — impressive, dark, and deceptively calm — and its name, "emperor," was earned by size and bearing rather than by venom.

In the dungeon, the emperor is the cave scorpion grown to sovereign scale: the same patience, the same high-carried tail, the same armoured advance, but with a weight that makes retreat seem pointless. Its folk character is therefore not the assassin of desert tales but the ruler of a small, dark territory — a creature that does not need to hurry, because everything in its ground is already, in its view, subject to it.

- **Defining traits:** heavy, armoured advance; crushing pincers first, tail sting second; holds territory and does not hurry.
- **Variants:** the West African emperor is the type; other large forest scorpions play the same role in other regions.

### Stag Beetle

The stag beetle is folklore's armoured knight in miniature. Its great, antler-like mandibles — the "stags' horns" that give it its name — made it a creature of duels and displays across Europe, where males fight each other by grappling with those jaws and trying to lift and throw their rival. Country lore treated it with a mixture of awe and suspicion: in some places it was said to carry lightning or to be drawn to storms, in others it was simply the largest and most impressive beetle a child could find, a living toy soldier with horns. Its larvae, which live for years in rotting wood before the adult ever emerges, gave it a second folk life as a creature of dead trees and old forests, a sign that a wood was ancient enough to have rot in it.

Real stag beetles are harmless to people despite their jaws — the great mandibles are for wrestling other males, not for biting — and they are powerful fliers for their bulk, clumsy in the air and formidable on the ground. A cave stag beetle is that duellist moved into the dark, where its instinct is unchanged: face the challenger squarely, lower the jaws, and try to throw it. Its lore is a lore of ritual combat, scaled down to the forest floor and scaled up again in the dungeon.

- **Defining traits:** plodding, armoured advance; faces challengers directly; lifts and throws with great mandibles.
- **Variants:** European, Asian, and North American stag beetles differ in size and jaw shape; the duelling habit is common to them all.

### Burrow Worm

The burrow worm belongs to a family of folk monsters that are mostly felt, not seen: the ground that moves, the ridge that travels, the eruption from below. Its most famous cousin is the Mongolian death worm of Central Asian legend, the allghoi khorkhoi, said to live in the Gobi sands and kill from beneath the surface. Closer cousins include the sandworms of desert tales, the great earthworms of folk exaggeration, and the very real giant earthworms of Australia and South America, which can reach lengths that make a casual observer doubt their eyes. The common thread is the terror of solid ground becoming unreliable — of the floor as a place where something else lives and hunts.

Real burrowing worms and insect larvae feed the legend from below. Earthworms, wireworms, and the larvae of many beetles and flies live their whole lives in soil, sensing vibration and moving through it with ease, and a person standing on soil is standing on their world. A burrow worm is that world made predatory: a creature that travels unseen, betrays itself only by a moving ridge, and erupts where its prey is standing. Its lore is therefore the lore of the earthquake in miniature — the ground is not safe, because the ground is inhabited.

- **Defining traits:** travels unseen beneath the floor; betrayed only by a moving ridge of disturbed earth; erupts with jaws open.
- **Variants:** desert sandworms, the Mongolian death worm, and giant earthworms are folk and real cousins; all share the unseen approach.

### Carrion Worm

Carrion worms are the dungeon's undertakers, and their folklore is the folklore of decay itself. Across cultures, worms in corpses and refuse were read as agents of dissolution — the things that finish what death began — and also, paradoxically, as signs of renewal, since the rot they hasten feeds new growth. Grave-worm tales warned against disturbing burials, not only because the dead should rest but because what lived in the grave was foul to touch. Medieval and early modern writers used the worm as an emblem of mortality: the body returns to the earth, and the earth, in the form of worms, takes it back.

Real carrion insects and worms do exactly that work, and they do it with chemistry as much as with jaws: the fluids of decay, the bacteria of rot, and the foul vapours of a carcass are part of how carrion feeders find food and defend it. A carrion worm is therefore not a hunter in the usual sense but a follower of death, drawn to where things have died and to the wounded who smell of it. Its horror is not speed or strength but association: where it is, something has already died, and something else may soon follow.

- **Defining traits:** sluggish crawl through rot and refuse; follows decay rather than hunting the healthy; foul spray and clinging corruption.
- **Variants:** grave worms, corpse worms, and maggot swarms are folk cousins; all share the link between rot and danger.

### Hornet Matriarch

The hornet matriarch is the queen of the insect world's most feared societies. Real hornets and wasps are eusocial: a single fertilised queen founds a nest, raises workers, and rules a colony that will defend her with its life. Folk tradition treated the hornet's nest as a small, armed kingdom — disturb it, and you face not one sting but an army acting as one creature — and the queen as the heart of that kingdom, larger than her workers, longer-lived, and the reason the nest exists at all. In some traditions hornets were also omens of war or of divine punishment, sent in swarms against enemies, as in the biblical telling where hornets drive out the inhabitants of a land before an army arrives.

The matriarch in the dungeon is that queen made personal: a great hornet who holds the air above her nest, directs her brood, and only dives herself when pressed. Her lore is the lore of the hive made visible — a single, royal body standing for a whole colony's will — and of the old warning that the most dangerous creature in a wasps' nest is not the sting you see coming, but the nest you did not see at all.

- **Defining traits:** commanding hover above the nest; directs her brood before diving herself; royal sting backed by a colony's defence.
- **Variants:** European hornets, Asian giant hornets, and paper-wasp queens differ in size and temper; the matriarch's role is the same in each.

### Ōmukade

Ōmukade — literally "giant centipede" — is a yōkai of Japanese folklore, and this entry is its home in the bestiary; its Japanese Yōkai overlap is noted here, not written a second time elsewhere. The great tale of the Ōmukade belongs to the hero Fujiwara no Hidesato, also called Tawara Tōda, "Lord Bag of Rice." Crossing the Seta Bridge over Lake Biwa, Hidesato met a great serpent who, in human form, begged him to rid the lake of a monstrous centipede that lived on Mount Mikami and devoured the Dragon King's kin, fish, beasts, and people alike. The creature was said to coil around the mountain itself, its many legs and glowing eyes descending like a thousand torches in the dark.

Hidesato's first two arrows bounced harmlessly off the centipede's armoured plates. Remembering the folk belief that human saliva is fatal to centipedes, he wet his third arrow with spittle, prayed to Hachiman, god of war and archery, and shot the creature through the head. He then hacked it apart, and the Dragon King rewarded him with gifts that included a bale of rice that never emptied — hence his nickname — and a sword later called the centipede-cutter. The tale, preserved in the Tawara Tōda Monogatari and in earlier chronicle tradition, is a hero story about a foe too large and too armoured for ordinary weapons, beaten by knowledge of a single weakness and by a hero calm enough to use it.

In wider yōkai tradition, the mukade is a mountain and cave creature, a man-eater, and a devourer of great serpents and dragons — a centipede grown past all natural scale until it is a rival to dragons themselves. Its appearance is consistently described: a vast, segmented body, armoured plates, countless legs moving in waves, and a head with venomous mandibles. That is the creature written here: not a mere large centipede but the mountain-coiling monster of the Lake Biwa tale, brought into the dungeon's depths.

- **Defining traits:** vast, armoured, many-legged body; coils around terrain and prey alike; venomous bite and crushing coil.
- **Variants:** the Mount Mikami Ōmukade of the Hidesato tale is the type; smaller mukade yōkai appear as mountain and house pests in other tales.
- **Abysmal take:** written here as a bestiary creature in its own right, with its Japanese Yōkai overlap noted in this entry only.

## Plants & Fungi

Plants that hunt are a paradox that has fascinated storytellers for centuries, because plants are supposed to be the background of a story, not its predator. Real carnivorous plants — flytraps, pitcher plants, sundews — eat insects because they live in poor soil and need the nitrogen. Folklore took that fact and enlarged it without permission: man-eating trees, vampire vines, and flowers that lure travellers to their deaths appear in travellers' tales, pulp fiction, and horror films, always playing on the same unease — that something rooted, patient, and apparently harmless might be waiting for you to come closer.

### Grotto Snapper

**Thin lore — director call.**

The Grotto Snapper has no tale of its own. It is a game-original snap-trap plant, and its honest ancestry is the snap trap itself: the Venus flytrap, whose paired leaves close in a fraction of a second when trigger hairs are touched twice, and the waterwheel plant, its aquatic cousin, which snaps shut on tiny water creatures in the same way. Those real plants are small, patient, and precise — they count touches before they commit, so that wind and rain do not waste a trap — and their snap is one of the fastest movements in the plant kingdom.

What the Snapper adds is scale and place: a grotto plant, rooted in a cave, whose jaw is large enough to matter to something bigger than a fly. That enlargement is the whole folk tradition of carnivorous plants in miniature — the step from "a plant that eats flies" to "a plant that eats you" — but no single story names a grotto snapper, and this entry does not pretend otherwise.

- **Defining traits:** rooted ambusher; jaw opens slowly as a warning, then slams shut; strikes only within its reach.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; flytrap and waterwheel forms are its real snap-trap cousins.

### Mandrake

The mandrake is the most storied plant in European folklore, and its story is a horror story about medicine. The plant itself, Mandragora officinarum, is a low, broad-leaved member of the nightshade family whose thick, forked root often resembles a small human figure, arms and legs and all. That resemblance made it a creature in the popular mind long before it was a medicine: a root that was half plant, half person, and wholly dangerous to handle. Greek and Roman physicians used mandrake wine as an anaesthetic, and medieval surgeons used mandrake-soaked sponges to put patients to sleep before operations — real, potent, and toxic medicine, whose alkaloids could heal, hallucinate, or kill depending on the dose.

The legend that outlived the medicine is the scream. Medieval herbals, drawing on older sources including the historian Josephus, warned that a mandrake pulled from the earth would shriek, and that anyone who heard the shriek would die or go mad. The prescribed remedy was to tie a hungry dog to the root, stop your own ears, and lure the dog away so that it, not you, pulled the plant and bore the sound. The plant was also said to shine at night, to flee from impure hands, to grow beneath gallows from the fluids of the hanged, and to serve witches as an ingredient in flying ointments. Shakespeare has Juliet fear "shrieks like mandrakes' torn out of the earth," and the screaming root has never left the imagination since — a plant that protests, in a human voice, at being torn from its home.

- **Defining traits:** human-shaped, shriek-rooted tuber; screams when pulled or disturbed; toxic, narcotic, and medicinal all at once.
- **Variants:** Mediterranean mandrakes, the American "mayapple" sometimes called mandrake, and the mandrakes of herbals and stage plays differ in botany but share the scream.
- **Abysmal take:** the shriek is the creature's voice and weapon here, as in folklore — a cry that staggers rather than a spell cast at a distance.

### Vampire Vine

The vampire vine belongs to the great pulp tradition of the man-eating plant, a tradition that grew up in the nineteenth century when travellers' tales, newspaper hoaxes, and early science fiction all reported — falsely, but vividly — trees and vines in Madagascar and elsewhere that seized travellers in tentacles and drank them dry. The most famous, the Ya-Te-Veo of a widely repeated 1870s account, was said to lure victims with sweet fruit, then wrap them in thorny, octopus-like tendrils. No such plant has ever been found, but the story stuck, because it combined two real plant behaviours that are strange enough on their own: the tendril that reaches, coils, and climbs, and the parasitic plant that taps into another living thing and feeds from it.

Real vines do coil, climb, and strangle — strangler figs, dodder, and the tendrils of peas and grapes all seize and hold — and real parasitic plants do drain their hosts. The vampire vine takes those two truths and adds the one folk addition that makes it a monster: it drinks not sap but blood, or in dungeon terms, the strength of whatever it coils around. Its lore is therefore a lore of exaggeration honestly owned: a real climbing habit, a real parasitic habit, and a folk appetite laid over both.

- **Defining traits:** creeping, questing tendrils; coils and holds its prey; drains strength while the coil holds.
- **Variants:** the Ya-Te-Veo and other man-eating trees of travellers' tales are its literary cousins; strangler figs and dodder are its real ones.

### Fungal Shambler

The fungal shambler walks out of two real horrors that only recently became folklore. The first is the mycelium itself: the vast, hidden network of fungal threads beneath soil and wood, which can be larger than any single mushroom and which feeds by digesting what it touches from the outside in. The second is the parasitic fungus that takes over an animal's body — most famously Ophiocordyceps, the "zombie-ant fungus," which infects an ant, steers it to a high place, kills it, and fruits from its corpse, spreading spores to the next victim. That fungus has become a modern folk monster in its own right, through nature documentaries and horror fiction alike, because it makes the shambling, mindless corpse a biological fact rather than a supernatural one.

Older folklore knew fungi as uncanny things, too: fairy rings where mushrooms grew in circles were said to mark fairy dances, and to step inside one was to risk enchantment or a lost night; toadstools were witches' seats and poisoners' tools. The shambler combines those traditions — the ring, the spore, and the body that keeps walking after something else has taken it over — into a single lurching figure, drifting spores as it moves, neither plant nor animal nor corpse, but the fungus wearing all three.

- **Defining traits:** lurching, uneven advance; spores drift from its body as it moves; bludgeons or bursts spores at close range.
- **Variants:** fairy-ring fungi, zombie-ant fungi, and the great mycelial networks of old forests are its real and folk cousins.

### Walking Oak

The walking oak is the tree that refuses to stay background, and its ancestry runs through the oldest tree lore in Europe. Sacred groves, world trees, and talking trees appear in Greek, Norse, and Celtic telling alike: the oak of Dodona that spoke oracles, Yggdrasil the world ash that held the nine realms, and the groves where cutting a tree could offend a god or a spirit. Tolkien's Ents gave the walking tree its modern form — ancient, slow, deliberate shepherds of the forest, who take a long time to say anything and longer to decide to act, but who, once roused, are unstoppable — and every walking tree since owes them a debt, whether it admits it or not.

Real oaks feed the legend with their own grandeur: they live for centuries, grow to enormous girth, and their roots tear and heave soil as they grow, cracking stone and lifting paths. A walking oak is therefore not a tree given magic for the sake of it, but a tree given the patience and the anger that old trees already seem to have. It moves slowly because it has never needed to hurry, and it strikes with limbs that have weathered centuries of storms. Its lore is the lore of the grove defending itself, after long silence, against the axe.

- **Defining traits:** slow, ponderous, root-tearing advance; sweeps great limbs in wide arcs; roused by harm to its grove.
- **Variants:** Ents, the talking trees of Dodona, and the world tree Yggdrasil are literary and mythic cousins; oak, ash, and yew forms differ in temper.

### Thornhound

**Thin lore — director call.**

The Thornhound has no single story behind it. It is a game-original plant-beast: a hound shape made of briar, bramble, and vine, hunting like a dog through undergrowth. Its honest cousins are twofold. On the plant side, the briar and the bramble have a folklore of their own — blackberry thickets as boundaries between the human world and the wild, thorn hedges as defences that keep things in as surely as they keep things out, and the hawthorn as a fairy tree not to be cut lightly. On the beast side, the hound of folklore — the black dog, the hellhound, the hunting hound of the Wild Hunt — is a creature that runs in packs, circles its prey, and cannot be outrun in its own territory.

The Thornhound joins those two traditions at their shared edge: the undergrowth where a dog can run and a person cannot, and where thorns do the work of teeth. That is a genuine folk image — the hedge that hunts — but no tale names a thornhound, and this entry does not invent one.

- **Defining traits:** loping, pack-hunting plant-beast; bounds through undergrowth on vine-wrapped limbs; rakes with thorns and tangles prey in vines.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; black dogs and hellhounds are its beast cousins, briar hedges its plant ones.

### Spore Servant

**Thin lore — director call.**

The Spore Servant is a game-original figure, and its honesty lies in naming what it borrows. Its body is a husk — a dead thing, or a thing that was once living — animated not by a soul or a spell in the old sense but by fungus. That idea has a modern folk life through the zombie-ant fungus and through horror fiction's "fungal zombie," but as a named creature the Spore Servant has no tale, no regional variant, and no older name. It is the Fungal Shambler's quieter cousin: where the Shambler is the fungus itself grown large and lurching, the Servant is what the fungus leaves behind to do its work, a puppet whose strings are spores.

Its real cousins are the same as the Shambler's — mycelial networks, parasitic fungi, and the fairy-ring tradition — but its specific image, the jerky, stop-start shamble of a body moved by something that is not its owner, belongs to puppet and golem lore as much as to fungus lore: a thing moved by another's will, without a will of its own. That is all the tradition it can honestly claim, and it is enough to recognise it by.

- **Defining traits:** slow, puppet-like shamble with jerky, stop-start steps; spore-covered body; grasps and presses close rather than striking from afar.
- **Variants:** none in tradition.

### Corpse Flower

The corpse flower is real, and its reality is stranger than most invented monsters. Amorphophallus titanum, the titan arum of Sumatra's rainforests, produces the largest unbranched flowering structure in the plant kingdom — a great, fleshy spike wrapped in a maroon spathe that can stand taller than a person — and it blooms only rarely, for a day or two, after years of growth. When it blooms, it heats its central spike to near body temperature and releases a stench of rotting flesh so powerful it can be smelled hundreds of metres away. That stench is a lure, not a weapon: carrion beetles and flesh flies, mistaking the bloom for a carcass, crawl inside to lay eggs and instead carry pollen from flower to flower.

Folk tradition added a second corpse flower, Rafflesia, a parasitic bloom of Southeast Asia that also smells of carrion and produces flowers of enormous size, and travellers' tales added a third, imaginary one: the flower that lures people, not insects, and closes on them. The dungeon's corpse flower is that third flower, honestly descended from the first two: the titan arum's heat, colour, and carrion scent, and Rafflesia's reputation as a bloom that looks like meat, enlarged until the lure works on something larger than a beetle. Its horror is not that it lies — it advertises exactly what it is, a thing that smells of death — but that prey comes to it anyway, drawn by curiosity, hunger, or the simple failure to believe a flower could be a trap.

- **Defining traits:** rooted lure; great bloom that opens in stages, releasing a heavy sweet-rot scent; petals close around prey that steps inside.
- **Variants:** the titan arum and Rafflesia are its real cousins; the man-eating bloom of travellers' tales is its literary one.
- **Abysmal take:** the real corpse flower does not trap or digest its pollinators; the engulfing bloom here is the folk exaggeration, not the botany.

## Aquatic & the Drowned Deep

Water in folklore is never neutral. It is the other world with a surface on it: the place the dead go, the place monsters live, and the place from which things return that should not have returned. Rivers have spirits, lakes have kings, and the deep sea has creatures too large for any map. The dungeon's flooded passages carry that tradition downward — water that is not scenery but a border, and things that strike from below it, from its edge, or from the dark beneath its surface.

### Cave Moray

The moray eel is one of the sea's oldest ambush predators, and its reputation has followed it from the Mediterranean to the Pacific. Roman aristocrats kept morays in fishponds and told stories — almost certainly exaggerated — of cruel owners feeding disobedient slaves to them. Greek and Roman writers treated the moray as a creature of holes and crevices, vicious when cornered and dangerous to handle, with a second set of jaws in its throat that lunges forward to drag prey down. Modern divers know a calmer truth: morays are shy, territorial eels that live in holes, show their heads to breathe and to watch, and bite when a hand or a fish comes too close to the hole they call home.

The cave moray is that eel moved into flooded stone, where its habits fit perfectly. A flooded crevice is a moray's hole with a roof on it, and a head showing at the opening, body hidden, is the moray's natural posture. Its lore is therefore the lore of the hole itself: the opening in the rock that looks empty, the head that was watching all along, and the lunge that covers the distance between "empty" and "bitten" in less time than it takes to step back.

- **Defining traits:** serpentine ambusher of flooded crevices; waits coiled with head out, body hidden; lunges from the opening.
- **Variants:** Mediterranean, Pacific, and freshwater morays differ in size and colour; the crevice habit is common to them all.

### Drowned Sailor

The drowned sailor is the ghost story that every seafaring culture tells, because every seafaring culture has drowned sailors to remember. Sailors lost at sea were, in many traditions, denied proper burial and therefore denied rest: they returned as spirits, as warnings, or as figures on the shore. Davy Jones and his locker gave the drowned a keeper and a destination; the Flying Dutchman gave them a ship that could never make port; and countless local tales told of drowned men seen walking the shore, dripping, seeking the living or seeking the way home. The common thread is not malice but incompleteness: a death without a grave, a journey without an arrival.

The dungeon's drowned sailor carries that incompleteness into flooded ground. It rises from water that should not be deep enough to drown in, drags itself forward with water streaming off it, and reaches for the living — not, in the oldest telling, out of hatred, but out of the same longing that makes any restless dead reach for the world it lost. Whether it means to drag its victim down with it or simply to be held, to be seen, to be buried, is a question the tales leave open, and the dungeon wisely leaves it open too.

- **Defining traits:** slow, dragging wade from flooded ground; water streaming; seizes and pulls toward the water.
- **Variants:** Davy Jones's crew, the Flying Dutchman's sailors, and shore-walking drowned men are folk cousins; all share the unburied, unarrived death.

### Giant Octopus

The giant octopus is the kraken with its mythology stripped back to the animal, and the animal is formidable enough. Real octopuses are among the most intelligent invertebrates known: they solve puzzles, use tools, squeeze through gaps barely wider than their beaks, change colour and texture to vanish against rock, and hunt with eight arms that can taste what they touch. Sailors' tales enlarged those gifts into the kraken, the island-sized beast of Norse and later seas, whose arms could seize ships and whose bulk could raise storms. The kraken was, for centuries, the sea's way of saying that the map ended here and something else began.

A giant octopus in a flooded chamber is that legend at a scale a dungeon can hold: boneless, squeezing through gaps, jetting across water, climbing wet walls, and wrapping prey in arms that are stronger than they look, drawing it toward a hard, parrot-like beak. Its lore is therefore a double lore — the real octopus's intelligence and flexibility, and the kraken's terror of arms rising from the deep — and the dungeon version needs no more than those two truths to be frightening.

- **Defining traits:** boneless, flowing movement; squeezes through gaps and climbs wet walls; wraps with tentacles and bites with a beak.
- **Variants:** the kraken is its mythic cousin; the giant Pacific octopus and other large species are its real ones.

### Deep Crab

Crabs have a smaller but stubborn place in folklore, usually as creatures of the shore's edge: sideways walkers, armoured scavengers, and, in some tales, guardians of thresholds between land and sea. The giant crab appears in travellers' tales and in the folklore of islands where coconut crabs — the largest land arthropods alive — really do climb trees, crack coconuts, and steal anything shiny left unguarded. In Japanese tradition, the Heikegani crab, whose shell markings resemble a samurai's face, was said to carry the souls of drowned warriors of the Heike clan, and fishermen threw those crabs back out of respect. The crab's lore is therefore a lore of armour, of sideways movement, and of the shore as a contested border.

The deep crab is that shore creature moved down into flooded chambers, where its habits still serve it: it scuttles sideways faster than it can turn, hugs the floor, and snaps a great claw with a force out of proportion to its patience. Its weakness, in every telling, is the same as its strength's price — it cannot turn quickly, and a foe that keeps it turning has already half beaten it.

- **Defining traits:** sideways scuttle, fast laterally and slow to turn; hugs the floor near the waterline; crushing claw snap.
- **Variants:** coconut crabs, Heikegani, and deep-sea giant crabs are folk and real cousins; all share the armour and the sideways gait.

### Lanternjaw

**Thin lore — director call.**

The Lanternjaw has no tale of its own. It is a game-original deep-water lurker, and its honest ancestry is the anglerfish: the deep-sea predator whose females dangle a glowing lure — a modified dorsal spine tipped with a sac of light-making bacteria — in front of a vast, fanged mouth, in waters where no sunlight reaches. In that darkness, light is the rarest thing there is, and any creature that makes its own light becomes, to everything around it, either food, a mate, or a curiosity worth investigating. The anglerfish exploits that curiosity with total patience: it hangs motionless, dangles the light, and waits for prey to come to the mouth rather than chasing prey through the dark.

The Lanternjaw takes that strategy and gives it a dungeon pool: a lone glow in dark water, a great jawed head below it, and a lunge for anything that approaches the light. That is the anglerfish's story, transferred from ocean trench to flooded passage, and it is a strong enough story to stand on. No folk tale names a lanternjaw, and this entry does not invent one for it.

- **Defining traits:** lurking drift in dark water; bioluminescent lure glows faintly and brightens before the strike; great jawed head surges upward.
- **Variants:** none in tradition; deep-sea anglerfishes are its real cousins, differing in lure shape and glow pattern by species.

### World Wurm

The World Wurm draws on the world-serpent motif of Norse myth, and its lore begins with that motif's most famous bearer — while remaining a bestiary creature in its own right, not the apex. In Norse telling, the world sea encircles Midgard, the world of humans, and in that sea lives a serpent so vast that it wraps the whole world and bites its own tail, forming a living boundary between order and chaos. That serpent is Jörmungandr, the Midgard Serpent, child of Loki, cast into the sea by Odin, grown until the world fit inside its coil. When it releases its tail, the old tales say, the sea will rise, the world will flood, and the final battle will begin.

The world-serpent idea is older and wider than Norse myth alone. Serpents that encircle the world, hold it up, or bite their own tails appear as the ouroboros of Egyptian and Greek tradition, as the great serpents of Hindu cosmology that support or churn the world ocean, and as the rainbow serpents of Australian tradition that shape land and water. In every version the serpent is not merely large; it is structural, a creature whose body is a geography, and whose movement is an event on the scale of floods and endings.

The World Wurm written here is that motif at dungeon scale: a vast, coiling serpent of flooded chambers and trench-deep water, whose body narrows the ground its prey can stand on and whose sweep covers a whole chamber at once. It is a world-serpent in miniature — a creature that makes a room feel like a world, and that coils around it the way its mythic cousins coil around everything.

- **Defining traits:** vast, coiling body; sweeps a wide arc or coils to crush; narrows the safe ground around it as it coils.
- **Variants:** Jörmungandr, the ouroboros, and the world-supporting serpents of Hindu and Australian tradition are mythic cousins.
- **Note:** the apex Jörmungandr is separate — its lore lives in design-notes/apex-bosses-lore-strategies.txt and wave4-apex-bosses.md, and is not redone here. This entry is the bestiary-row World Wurm only.

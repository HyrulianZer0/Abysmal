# Bestiary lore — Goblin-Kin & Cave Folk, Orcs, Trolls & Giant-Kin, Humans & the Damned Living

This is the lore home for 38 creatures: 12 Goblin-Kin & Cave Folk, 15 Orcs, Trolls & Giant-Kin, and 11 Humans & the Damned Living. It is folklore and story first, not a list of game rules: no numbers and no mechanics appear here. Where a creature is mainly a modern fantasy invention rather than a figure from an old tradition, that is said plainly instead of dressing it up with an invented mythology.

Overlap notes matter for counting, not for duplication. Redcap, Spriggan, and Púca are written here, with their Celtic and British Isles overlap noted; Frost Giant and Fire Giant are written here, with their Norse overlap noted. Each creature has exactly one entry in this document. Apex uniques are not covered here; their lore lives elsewhere.

## Goblin-Kin & Cave Folk

The small folk of European story are not one creature with one temperament. They are a whole crowded borderland between house and wild, help and harm: household spirits who will work all night for a bowl of milk and turn vicious if insulted, mine spirits who knock in the dark, bogeys invented to frighten children into obedience, and, in later fantasy, whole tribes of small warriors who live by raiding, cunning, and numbers. What unites them is scale and cunning rather than strength. They are the folk you bargain with, avoid, or outwit — rarely the folk you meet in open battle and expect to win by size alone.

### Kobold

The kobold is a German household spirit, a *hausgeist*, whose name is tangled up with the whole goblin family. Scholars have traced it toward the Greek *kobalos*, a rogue or trickster, and toward older German words for a spirit of a single room or hearth; the English word goblin and the Dutch *kabouter* are often treated as cousins, if not descendants, of the same root. Belief in kobolds is attested from at least the thirteenth century, when German peasants carved small effigies for their homes, and the figure looks like a survival of older pagan household custom carried on quietly after Christianisation. Famous named kobolds of the house kind include King Goldemar, Heinzelmann, and Hödekin, the little-hat spirit whose story of service and retribution was already old when the Grimms retold it.

There are, broadly, three kobold haunts. The house kobold does chores in secret and plays malicious tricks when insulted or neglected; the mine kobold, sometimes called a *Bergmännlein*, haunts underground workings, mimicking miners at work and, in miners' lore, swapping good ore for worthless or poisonous rock — the metal cobalt takes its name from this blamed sprite, because its ores polluted the silver miners sought. The third kind, the *Klabautermann*, lives aboard ships and helps sailors. In appearance the house kind wears peasant clothing, the mine kind is hunched and ugly, and the ship kind smokes a pipe in sailor's dress. Later writers, from Martin Luther to Olaus Magnus, darkened the kobold's reputation by tying it to devils and witchcraft, but its older core is ambivalent: helpful until slighted, mischievous until feared.

- **Variants:** house kobold; mine kobold (*Bergmännlein*); ship kobold (*Klabautermann*); named figures such as Heinzelmann and Hödekin.
- **Abysmal take:** in Abysmal the kobold's core is gadget-tinker — traps and small devices are its signature — its colour is open, and dragonkin is not required. That is game canon, not folklore: the old kobold is a house and mine spirit, not a little dragon.

### Goblin

Goblin is less a single creature than an umbrella word that has swallowed centuries of small, ugly, vaguely humanoid sprites. It reaches English through the French *gobelin* and medieval Latin *gobelinus*, with the Greek *kobalos* (rogue, trickster) behind it and the German kobold alongside it. Its first written appearances are ghostly rather than warlike: Orderic Vitalis, writing before 1141, describes a spirit haunting the region of Évreux, and later medieval goblins attach themselves to houses much like poltergeists, moving things, making noises, and generally making a household uneasy. By the time the word settles into Middle English it is already loose, covering almost anything small, troublesome, and not quite human.

What a goblin looks like and how dangerous it is therefore depends entirely on who is telling the tale. In older folklore it is a wandering sprite, sometimes helpful, more often a nuisance, and only sometimes truly malevolent. George MacDonald's 1872 novel *The Princess and the Goblin* gave later fantasy its chaotic, filthy, thieving mountain goblins, and Tolkien then made the crucial modern move of treating his goblins and his orcs as the same kind of creature under different names. That literary line, more than any single folktale, is why the modern goblin is a tribal cave-dweller who raids in packs. The older root, though, is the household trickster: small, greedy, easily offended, and best dealt with by keeping a tidy house and a wary eye.

- **Variants:** house-haunting sprite; mountain and mine goblin of literary fantasy; the hobgoblin as a larger, friendlier cousin (see Hobgoblin).
- **Traits that recur:** small stature, pointed ears in later art, greed for food and shiny things, a love of dark, underground, or ruined places.

### Goblin Sneak

**thin lore — director call.** There is no standalone figure called the Goblin Sneak in folklore. Sneaking, lurking in shadows, and striking from behind are behaviours attached to goblins in general — and to the whole family of bogeys, boggarts, and household sprites who work unseen — but no named tradition, tale cycle, or regional variant gives this creature its own story, appearance, or origin. The Sneak is best understood as a role within goblin lore rather than a separate being: the goblin as it appears when a household or a traveller only ever glimpses it, hears it moving in the dark, and finds something stolen or broken afterwards. Any fuller backstory for it would have to be invented for the game, and this entry deliberately does not invent one.

### Goblin Shaman

The Goblin Shaman is a fantasy composite rather than a figure from an old tale: goblins in folklore are not generally organised around shamans, priests, or spell-casters. Its two halves do each have real roots. The first is the goblin itself, the small trickster spirit described above. The second is the shaman, a figure found across Siberian, Central Asian, and many other traditions: a ritual specialist who mediates with spirits, often with drums, rattles, fetishes made of bone and feather, and trance or chant. Fantasy literature and games then fused the two, giving goblin tribes a spiritual leader who rallies the pack and works crude magic, in contrast to the disciplined priesthoods of larger peoples. That fusion is the whole of this creature's tradition. It has no named folktale of its own, and its bone beads, staff, and rallying gestures are game and genre conventions drawn from the shamanic image, not quotations from a particular myth.

### Hobgoblin

The hobgoblin is the friendlier, larger cousin in the goblin family, and in older English folklore it is a very different creature from the disciplined warrior of modern fantasy. The *hob* in its name is a familiar form of Robin or Robert, a rustic nickname, and the hobgoblin of tradition is essentially Robin Goodfellow, or Puck: a household sprite who does good work by stealth at night — threshing, churning, tidying — provided it is fed bread and milk, and who turns to mischief if mocked or left unpaid. Shakespeare's Puck in *A Midsummer Night's Dream* is the most famous literary version of this figure: swift, jesting, and fond of leading travellers astray, but not a soldier. Scottish brownies belong to the same household-helping family, and the hobgoblin sits naturally beside them.

The change from household helper to armoured tribesman happened in modern fantasy, where hobgoblins were recast as bigger, tougher, more organised goblins — the officers and veterans of goblin warbands. That recasting is so complete that many readers now meet the warrior first and never meet the house sprite. Both versions share a core, though: the hobgoblin is a goblin grown larger and more consequential, whether its consequence is a barn cleaned overnight or a shield-wall held in a cave mouth. The older lore explains the name; the newer lore explains the sword.

- **Variants:** Robin Goodfellow / Puck; the helpful hob of northern farmhouses, such as the hob of Runswick Bay, said to cure whooping cough; the modern fantasy hobgoblin warrior.

### Bugbear

The bugbear begins as a word for terror, not as a species. It comes from the old word *bug*, a hobgoblin or frightening spirit, joined to *bear*: a dreadful bear-like apparition, the thing that ate naughty children in nursery warnings, and, by extension, anything needlessly frightening — the modern weakened sense of a bugbear as a pet peeve is the ghost of that older dread. It is kin to the bogle, the bogeyman, and the Welsh *bwgwl*, terror itself, and it belongs to the language parents and storytellers used to give shapeless fear a shape in the dark. A bugbear in this older sense has no tribe, no warband, and no fixed anatomy beyond being big, hairy, and best avoided.

Modern fantasy, above all the tabletop tradition, rebuilt the bugbear as a large, stealthy goblinoid: a hulking cousin of the goblin who stalks silently despite its bulk and mauls its prey at close quarters. That version borrows the old name's menace and the bear's bulk, but it is a twentieth-century invention laid over a much older fright-word. The continuity is the ambush in the dark: whether it is a nursery bogey or a cave predator, the bugbear is the thing you do not hear coming.

### Boggart

The boggart is a supernatural being of northern England, above all Lancashire and Yorkshire, with written mentions from the 1500s and a flood of material from the mid-nineteenth to the early twentieth centuries — so much, in fact, that modern scholarship has called it Britain's most understudied supernatural being. Regional glossaries most often define it simply as a ghost, then as a hobgoblin or goblin, and the word sits in a family with the Scottish *bogle*, the English *bogey* and *bogeyman*, and the Welsh *bwg*, terror. Boggarts are intensely local: they attach to a house, a farm, a stretch of lane, or a particular hollow, and their stories often begin or end with someone racing through the dark to safety, as in the Lancashire radical Samuel Bamford's youthful encounter.

What a boggart does depends on the telling, and that variety is the point. Some are mere mischief-makers, pelting travellers with stale bread, smashing crockery, moving stones and scaffolding by night — Lancashire lore credits goblins of this kind with deciding the sites of churches at Rochdale, Burnley, and Samlesbury by removing builders' work. Others are frankly hostile, haunting a house until the family leaves, or, in the famous Hothersall Hall story, becoming so troublesome that they must be tricked or driven out. A boggart is not a tribe and not a species with a single shape; it is what a place's fear and mischief condense into, and it is best understood as a haunting with a personality: stubborn, local, and very hard to be rid of once it has settled.

- **Variants:** Lancashire house boggart; lane and field boggart; the boggart as ghost in regional glossaries; the boggart as hobgoblin or Puck-like trickster.
- **Kin:** bogle, bogeyman, bugbear, brownie — the same northern family under different local names.

### Redcap

*Also Celtic & British Isles — written here per assignment, overlap noted.*

The redcap, also called the powrie or dunter, is a malevolent goblin of Anglo-Scottish Border folklore, haunting ruined castles along the border, especially those that were scenes of tyranny or wicked deeds. It is described as a sturdy old man with red eyes, large teeth, and taloned hands, wearing iron-shod boots and carrying a pikestaff, and its name comes from its cap: the redcap must soak its cap in the blood of its victims, and if the blood dries, it dies. That single grim rule makes it one of folklore's most honest monsters — its survival is literally measured in murder, and it waits in its ruin for travellers to stray inside, flinging stones and falling on them with its staff.

The most famous redcap story belongs to Lord William de Soulis, whose familiar, Robin Redcap, was said to serve a lord practising witchcraft at Hermitage Castle; folklore claims de Soulis was boiled alive by an enraged mob, though history records him imprisoned for conspiracy and dying in mysterious circumstances. A rarer, gentler telling from Perthshire places a redcap in a room at Grantully Castle bestowing good fortune on those who see or hear it, a reminder that even this killer has a benign cousin in the wider red-capped family — the Dutch *kabouter*, for instance, is far closer to a brownie. But the Border redcap is the bloody one, and it stays bloody: a creature that cannot stop killing without dying itself.

### Spriggan

*Also Celtic & British Isles — written here per assignment, overlap noted.*

Spriggans are Cornish fairy folk, their name drawn from *spyrysyon*, spirits, and they are the guardians of the old places: cairns, barrows, standing stones, and buried treasure. They are usually pictured as small, grotesque, wrinkled old men, thoroughly ill-tempered — in Cornish tradition they are the ghosts of giants, shrunken but not tamed, and they guard their hoards with a spite that matches their size. Cross a spriggan's guarded spot and it will not merely chase you; it will swell, growing to giant stature in a moment, and use that borrowed bulk to drive you off. That sudden growth is the spriggan's signature, and it is why the creature is both comic and frightening: the little guardian you laughed at is, a heartbeat later, the giant whose barrow you were robbing.

Spriggans also have a weather-working, mischievous side in Cornish stories, raising storms and blighting crops when offended, and they are sometimes blamed for stealing children or swapping them, like other fairy folk. But their heart is guardianship. Where other fairies dance or bargain, the spriggan watches. Its ugliness is part of the warning: this is not a fairy to charm or befriend, but a warden left over from a larger, older world, still doing its job long after the giants themselves are gone.

### Gremlin

The gremlin is the youngest creature in this family, and the only one born in the age of machines. The word appears in Royal Air Force slang from the 1920s — oral use among pilots in Malta, the Middle East, and India is dated to 1923, with a first printed use possibly in the journal *Aeroplane* in 1929 — and it became famous in the Second World War, when British and then American aircrews blamed gremlins for every unexplained engine trouble, instrument failure, and de-icer fault. The creatures were imagined as tiny, unseen saboteurs who got into engines, meddled with flight controls and bombing sights, and generally vexed the men who flew. The name itself is of uncertain origin, plausibly from Old English *gremman*, to vex or enrage, or from a Cornish or Devon dialect word for a little evil spirit.

Roald Dahl, himself an RAF pilot, made the gremlin literary in his 1943 children's book *The Gremlins*, in which a pilot whose Hurricane is destroyed over the Channel persuades the gremlins to turn their sabotage against the enemy instead. Later popular culture — above all the 1984 film *Gremlins* — kept the name but changed the creature, turning a wartime explanation for mechanical failure into a horde of small monsters. The folklore core, though, is smaller and sadder and funnier: the gremlin is what airmen invented to blame when a machine they depended on failed for no reason they could see, a goblin updated for the cockpit. Pilots even carried guardian bells, meant to trap or drive off gremlins with their ringing — a charm against a creature that was, from the start, a charm against helplessness.

### Púca

*Also Celtic & British Isles — written here per assignment, overlap noted.*

The púca (Irish for spirit or ghost; also *pwca* in Welsh, *pooka*, *phouka*, and the English *puck*) is a shapeshifting trickster of Celtic, English, and Channel Islands folklore. It most often takes the form of a domestic animal — above all a dark horse, but also a goat, dog, or rabbit — or appears as a human with telltale animal features: large ears, fur, or a tail. It is not, in most tales, a straightforward villain. It is capricious, playful, and sometimes kindly, but its play has teeth: the classic púca story is the wild ride, in which a lone night traveller is invited or tricked onto the creature's back and carried at terrifying speed over hedges, ditches, and rough country, only to be dumped, shaken and unharmed, at dawn. The rider learns the lesson; the púca gets its laugh.

Place-names keep the púca's geography: Carrig-a-Phooka near Macroom, Castle Pooka near Doneraile, and the island of Melaan at the mouth of the Kenmare river, dreaded by sailors and fishers at night, are all púca places in Irish tradition. The púca is also tied to the harvest: November 1, Samhain, was its day, when offerings were left in the fields and anything left unharvested after that date was said to belong to the púca and to be spoiled by it. Unlike the redcap, which must kill, or the spriggan, which must guard, the púca must only play — and its play is a reminder that the fairy world does not share human ideas of fairness, only its own sense of a joke well told.

- **Variants:** dark horse (the commonest form); goat, as in the Pooka of Murroe; human with animal features; the Welsh *pwca* and English *puck* as linguistic kin.
- **Days and places:** Samhain (November 1) as the púca's day; Carrig-a-Phooka and other Irish púca sites.

### Goblin Champion

**thin lore — director call.** There is no traditional figure called the Goblin Champion. Folklore gives us goblin kings and chiefs in scattered stories — the Goblin King of *The Princess and the Goblin* and his literary descendants, for instance — but no named champion, no rite by which a goblin earns that title, and no regional variant with its own tale. A champion, in the sense of a veteran warrior who leads a pack and embodies its strength, is a role invented by fantasy games and fiction to give a goblin tribe a leader worth fearing in a fight. The goblin lore behind it is real — pack behaviour, greed, cunning, and a willingness to follow a strong leader — but the Champion as a distinct creature is a game convention, and this entry does not pretend otherwise.

## Orcs, Trolls & Giant-Kin

This family is the heavy end of the old stories, and its members blur into one another in ways that are themselves part of the lore. The same Old English and Old Norse words slide between ogre, giant, troll, and demon; the same monster appears as Grendel in one poem, as a *þurs* in another, and as an ettin in a border ballad. What holds the family together is not a single origin but a single fear: something human-shaped, larger than a human, stronger than a human, and living just beyond the farmed valley — in the mountains, the marsh, the cave, or the waste — that does not keep human law. Later fantasy sorted that fear into ranks, from orc foot-soldier to giant king, but the old fear was messier, and this section keeps some of that mess.

### Orc

The modern orc is, overwhelmingly, Tolkien's creature, but its name is older and stranger. Tolkien took the word from Old English *orc*, found in *Beowulf* in the compound *orcneas* — a tribe of monstrous beings, descendants of Cain, listed alongside elves and *eotenas* (giants) as the evil broods born after Cain's crime. Glossaries equated *orc* with *þyrs*, ogre, and *heldeofol*, hell-devil, and the word is generally thought to derive from Latin *Orcus*, the Roman god of the underworld. Tolkien himself said he chose it largely for its sound, and in a note to later editions of *The Hobbit* he explained that his goblins and his orcs were the same creatures, *orc* being the hobbits' form of the name.

Before Tolkien, George MacDonald's goblins in *The Princess and the Goblin* (1872) supplied the chaotic, filthy, thieving mountain folk who live underground and hate daylight, and William Morris's trollish Huns in *The Roots of the Mountains* (1889) supplied something of their warlike cast. Tolkien fused those influences into a brutish, aggressive, numerous people, bred or corrupted by a dark lord — his own accounts of their origin vary between corrupted elves, corrupted men, and creatures bred by Morgoth — and set them as the standing enemy of elves and men. Almost all later fantasy orcs descend from that fusion, whether they follow it closely or deliberately subvert it. The old *orcneas* of *Beowulf* is the name's root; the modern orc is Tolkien's tree grown from it.

### Orc Berserker

The Orc Berserker is a fantasy fusion of two traditions. The first is the orc itself, the tribal warrior above. The second is the Norse *berserkr*, the bear-shirt warrior of the sagas: a fighter who wore a bear skin — or, in the wolf variant, a wolf skin, as the *úlfhéðnar* — and worked himself into the *berserkergang*, a frenzied state in which, the sagas say, he felt neither cold, injury, nor fear, howled, bit his shield, and could not tell friend from foe. The *Ynglinga Saga* describes berserkers going into battle in bear skins, and later explanations have ranged from ritual possession by the animal's spirit to the effects of psychoactive plants, to simple literary exaggeration of elite shock troops.

No old text describes an orc berserker as such; the combination belongs to modern fantasy, which took the berserker's frenzy — the charge, the flurry, the refusal to stop — and gave it to its most warlike goblinoid. The lore behind it is therefore the berserker's, not the orc's: a warrior who trades judgement for terror, whose strength is real but whose frenzy is also his weakness, because a fighter who cannot stop cannot choose. That tension, between awe at the frenzy and horror at its blindness, is what the sagas preserve, and it is what the fantasy berserker inherits.

### Half-Ogre

**thin lore — director call.** The half-ogre has no folktale of its own. Ogres in folklore are not generally given families, marriages, or half-human children with their own customs; they appear as solitary eaters of children and travellers, and when a tale needs a creature between human and ogre it usually gives us a giant's son, a changeling, or a hero of unusual size, not a distinct people called half-ogres. The half-ogre is a fantasy-game category, a way of grading brute strength between orc and ogre, and its lore is therefore borrowed: it takes the ogre's bulk and appetite in a smaller, faster frame, and its parentage is a premise rather than a story. No regional variant, named half-ogre, or traditional tale is recorded for it, and this entry does not invent one.

### Ogre

The ogre is the fairy-tale giant at its most domestic and most frightening. The French word *ogre* is thought to derive from Latin *Orcus*, god of the underworld, and the creature crystallised in French and Italian fairy tales of the seventeenth century and earlier: a large, grotesque, man-eating humanoid, often stupid, often rich, and almost always hungry for children. Charles Perrault's tales give us ogres as household tyrants; Giambattista Basile's *Pentamerone* (1634) gives us ogres as brutish, easily outwitted villains; and the giant at the top of the beanstalk in *Jack and the Beanstalk* — sniffing out Englishmen, hoarding gold, and eating whoever climbs into his castle — is the ogre in its most famous English form. The ogre's wife, who hides the hero and warns him, is nearly as traditional as the ogre himself.

Behind the fairy-tale ogre stand older giants: the Greek Cyclops, the Norse *jötnar*, and the Anglo-Saxon *þyrs*, all large, isolated, man-eating or man-threatening beings who live beyond settled land. The ogre is that ancient giant brought indoors, given a castle or a cottage, a wife, a larder, and a nose for children. Its defining traits have barely changed in four centuries: great size, great appetite, low cunning rather than high intellect, and a vulnerability to the small and clever — the child, the tailor, the youngest son — who outwits what it cannot outfight.

- **Variants:** the French fairy-tale ogre; the beanstalk giant; the Italian ogre of Basile; the ogre's wife as helper and warning voice.
- **Kin:** Cyclops, troll, giant — the same large man-eater under different names.

### Marsh Troll

**thin lore — director call.** There is no traditional creature specifically called the Marsh Troll with its own tale cycle. Trolls of Scandinavian folklore live in mountains, rocks, forests, and caves, and some water spirits — the *nøkken*, the *grindylow*, Jenny Greenteeth — haunt marshes, bogs, and still water, but no named marsh troll stands between them with a story of its own. The Marsh Troll is a habitat variant: a troll imagined in wet ground, where its bulk, patience, and long reach suit a creature that waits half-submerged and rises when prey comes near. Its lore is therefore troll lore applied to a marsh, plus the wider folklore of bog creatures that drag the unwary under. A fuller marsh-troll mythology would be invention, and this entry does not supply one.

### Troll

The troll is one of the oldest and slipperiest words in northern folklore. In Old Norse, *troll* covers a startling range: a mountain-dweller, a witch, an abnormally strong or ugly person, an evil spirit, a ghost, a heathen demi-god, a demon, and even a berserker. The *jötnar*, the giants of Norse myth, are often called trolls, and the trolls of later Scandinavian folktales are their smaller, uglier descendants: huge, misshapen, and usually evil creatures who live in caves and mountains, raid outlying farms at night for sheep, horses, and people, and eat what they steal. Icelandic saga trolls are much the same — mountain and cave dwellers, hostile to settled folk, and dangerous precisely because they are both strong and cunning.

Two traits dominate later troll lore. The first is sunlight: in many tales, and famously in Tolkien, trolls caught by dawn turn to stone, a trait that makes them creatures of night and cave. The second is their place in the landscape: trolls live in rocks and mountains, guard bridges and passes in later stories (the bridge troll of *Three Billy Goats Gruff* is a Norwegian folktale troll), and embody the wild land's hostility to travellers. Modern fantasy has added a third trait, rapid healing, which is not a traditional troll trait — the old troll is hard to kill because it is big and tough and lives where you cannot easily reach it, not because its wounds close before your eyes. That modern addition is noted here so it is not mistaken for folklore.

- **Variants:** Norse *jötunn* / *þurs* as troll; Scandinavian folktale mountain and cave troll; bridge troll of *Three Billy Goats Gruff*; Tolkien's stone-by-daylight troll.
- **Traits that recur:** great size and strength, ugliness, cave and mountain homes, hostility to farms and travellers, danger after dark.

### Orc Shaman

Like the Goblin Shaman, the Orc Shaman is a fantasy composite, not a figure from an old tale. Its shamanic half is real: shamans across Siberian, Central Asian, and other traditions are ritual specialists who deal with spirits through drumming, chanting, fetishes, and trance, and war leaders in many cultures have kept ritual specialists close to bless weapons, read omens, and hearten warriors before battle. Its orcish half is Tolkien's tribal warrior. Fantasy fused them into a warband's spiritual engine — the figure who beats the war drum, raises the fetish, and makes the warband fight harder than it otherwise would — because a tribe that only charges is a simpler enemy than a tribe that also believes. No single myth gives this creature a name, a story, or a rite of its own; its tradition is the general history of war-magic and spirit mediation, applied to orcs.

### Cave Troll

The Cave Troll is a habitat variant of the troll, not a separate traditional figure. Trolls in Scandinavian folklore already live in caves and mountains — the cave is one of their classic homes, alongside the rock and the forest — so a cave troll is, in folklore terms, simply a troll encountered where trolls are expected. Later fantasy, above all Tolkien's cave troll of Moria, gave the cave troll a distinct image: larger, paler, more brutish than its mountain cousins, a creature of total darkness that throws stones and wields a club in the deep places under mountains. That image is powerful, but it is literary rather than folkloric: no regional tale cycle distinguishes cave trolls from other trolls by custom, name, or origin. Its lore is troll lore, told underground.

### Ettin

The ettin is a giant whose name preserves a whole history of northern monsters. It comes from Old English *eoten*, the equivalent of Old Norse *jötunn*, the giant race descended from Ymir, and it survives in Scots and northern English as *ettin*, meaning a giant or ogre. *Beowulf* lists *eotenas* among the evil broods descended from Cain, alongside elves and *orcneas*, and the word's kinship with *ent* — Tolkien's tree-giants take their name from the same Old English root — shows how widely the giant family spread through English story. In border ballads and folktales the ettin is a large, dangerous, often stupid giant who lives in a castle or a wild place and must be outwitted or slain by a hero.

The two-headed ettin, familiar from C. S. Lewis's Ettinsmoor in Narnia and from later fantasy, is a later elaboration: traditional ettins are not generally described as two-headed, and the second head is a fantasy way of making the giant harder to surprise and harder to kill. The older ettin needs no second head to be frightening; it is already the giant at the edge of the map, the *eoten* who fought against God in *Beowulf*'s telling and who, in Norse myth, fought against the gods themselves. Size, appetite, and a home beyond the settled world are its true marks.

### Cyclops

The Cyclops is a one-eyed giant of Greek myth, and its most famous member is Polyphemus, son of Poseidon, in Homer's *Odyssey*. The Cyclopes in Homer are lawless, uncivilised herdsmen who live in caves, keep sheep, and recognise no law, council, or hospitality: when Odysseus and his men enter Polyphemus's cave and help themselves to his food, the giant returns, seals the entrance with a stone so vast that two dozen wagons could not move it, and eats Odysseus's men one by one. Odysseus escapes by getting Polyphemus drunk, blinding his single eye with a heated stake while he sleeps, and slipping out clinging to the bellies of the giant's sheep, having first told the giant his name is Nobody — so that when Polyphemus cries for help, his neighbours hear that Nobody has hurt him and leave him alone. The story is also a lesson in *xenia*, the Greek law of hospitality, which Polyphemus violates by eating his guests instead of feeding them.

Earlier Greek tradition gives us other Cyclopes: the smith Cyclopes — Brontes, Steropes, and Arges — who forged Zeus's thunderbolts, and the wall-building Cyclopes credited with the massive masonry of Mycenae. Later writers kept the single eye and the giant stature but varied the temperament, from craftsman to monster. The Cyclops endures because its image is so economical: one eye, one cave, one appetite, and a hero who must win by wit because he cannot win by strength.

- **Variants:** Homer's herdsman Cyclopes, above all Polyphemus; the smith Cyclopes who forged the thunderbolts; the Cyclopean builders of Mycenaean walls.

### Hill Giant

The Hill Giant is a fantasy rank of giant rather than a figure from a single myth, but its roots run through the whole giant tradition. In Norse myth, giants live in the hills and mountains of Jötunheimr; in English folktales, giants live on hills, in castles on high ground, or at the top of beanstalks, and heroes climb to meet them. The hill giant of fantasy takes that geography and makes it a type: a giant of the open high ground, less cunning than a stone giant, less elemental than a frost or fire giant, but larger and stronger than a troll, armed with a tree limb or a boulder and dangerous because it can see you coming from a long way off and can throw things at you all the way down.

Its lore is therefore the general lore of giants applied to hills: giants as the older, wilder inhabitants of the land, pushed into the high and waste places by gods and men, still raiding farms and roads when hunger or anger moves them. The English giant of *Jack and the Beanstalk* and the Cornish giants of the old tales — Cormoran of St Michael's Mount, for instance — are hill giants in all but name: huge, local, and tied to a particular height that travellers learn to avoid.

### Stone Giant

The Stone Giant is, like the Hill Giant, a fantasy type built from older material, but its older material is unusually rich. In Norse myth, giants are made of the stuff of the world itself: Ymir's body becomes the earth, the sea, and the sky, and the *bergrisar*, mountain giants, are giants of rock in all but name. In folktales across Europe, giants turn to stone — trolls caught by daylight, giants tricked into standing still until dawn — and stone giants, living statues, and animated mountains blur together in stories of enchanted castles and cursed valleys. A stone giant is that blur given a name: a giant that looks like part of the landscape until it moves, throws rocks because rocks are what it is made of, and endures blows that would fell a flesh giant because its skin is stone.

The fantasy stone giant also inherits the giant's classic role as a guardian of high, rocky places, and its patience: where a hill giant charges and a frost giant storms, a stone giant waits. That patience is folkloric, too. The troll under the bridge, the giant asleep in his castle, the mountain that is not a mountain — all are stone giants before the name existed, and all teach the same lesson: look carefully at the landscape, because it may be looking back.

### Frost Giant

*Also Norse & Germanic — written here per assignment, overlap noted.*

The frost giants are the *jötnar* of Norse myth in their most famous form. They descend from Ymir, the first giant, who was born in the void of Ginnungagap where the ice of Niflheim met the heat of Muspelheim; while Ymir slept, a male and a female grew from the sweat under his arm and a six-headed son grew from his feet, and from those children came the race of *hrímþursar*, rime-giants or frost giants. Odin and his brothers slew Ymir and made the world from his body — his blood the seas, his skull the sky — and all the frost giants drowned in that blood except Bergelmir, who escaped in a wooden ark and from whom later giants descend. The frost giants live in Jötunheimr, a wintry land of heavy snow, infertile soil, and great forests, separated from Asgard by the river Ifingr, and they are the standing enemies and occasional uneasy kin of the gods: Thor's hammer was made to fight them, Loki's mother Laufey and his wife Angrboða are jötnar, and at Ragnarök the giants will march against the gods.

A caution belongs here. In the old texts, *jötunn* is not an ice-elemental specialist: frost giant is often simply a synonym for giant, a descendant of Ymir, and individual jötnar embody fire, wind, sea, and other powers as well as cold. The frost giant as a creature of freezing breath and ice armour is largely a modern fantasy specialisation, which takes the rime-giant's wintry home and makes it a weapon. The old frost giant is dangerous because it is a giant, ancient, and hostile to the gods' order — not because it breathes winter, though winter is certainly where it lives.

- **Notable figures:** Ymir, the first giant; Bergelmir, survivor of the flood of Ymir's blood; Thrym, king of the frost giants in later tales, who stole Thor's hammer; the jötnar as a whole, enemies of the Æsir at Ragnarök.

### Ogre Mage

**thin lore — director call.** The Ogre Mage has no traditional folktale. Its closest ancestor is the Japanese *oni* as adapted by modern fantasy: in Japanese lore, oni are horned ogre-demons, sometimes spellcasters and sometimes mere brutes, and the tabletop tradition's ogre mage — a large ogre with magical powers, shapeshifting, and a taste for domination — draws on that oni image filtered through the Western ogre. But no Japanese tale gives us a creature called an ogre mage with its own story, and no European tale gives an ogre a wizard's repertoire. The Ogre Mage is a game invention, a way of putting a spellcaster's threat into an ogre's body, and its lore is the ogre's bulk plus the oni's magic, combined by design rather than by tradition.

### Fire Giant

*Also Norse & Germanic — written here per assignment, overlap noted.*

The fire giants are the sons of Muspell, the *Múspellsmegir* or *eldjötnar*, who live in Muspelheim, the southern realm of fire at the edge of Ginnungagap. Their lord is Surtr, the black one, who stands at Muspelheim's border with a flaming sword and guards the realm of heat, ash, and flame. In the old texts the fire giants are not described in the detail lavished on the frost giants; their role is concentrated and terrible, and it comes at the end. At Ragnarök, Surtr will lead his sons from the south across the battlefield of Vígríðr, his sword shining brighter than the sun, and he will fight the god Freyr and set the world itself on fire. The fire giants are, in this telling, not a tribe with customs but an ending: the fire that was present at the world's beginning, kept in the south, returning to consume the world at its close.

As with the frost giants, a caution applies. The old texts call Surtr's people the sons of Muspell, not a generic elemental type, and they do not give every fire giant a flame breath or a burning sword; Surtr's sword is his, and the burning of the world is the host's work, not each giant's personal trick. Modern fantasy has generalised Surtr into a whole kind of fire giant, just as it generalised Ymir's children into frost giants. The Norse core is narrower and grimmer: one lord, one realm, one final fire.

- **Notable figures:** Surtr, lord of Muspelheim, bearer of the flaming sword, slayer of Freyr at Ragnarök; the sons of Muspell as a host, not a tribe with individual tales.

## Humans & the Damned Living

The most unsettling family in this bestiary is the one that is not a monster family at all. Brigands, grave robbers, cultists, witches, and fallen knights are human, and their horror is human horror: greed, fanaticism, desperation, and the bargains people make when law, hunger, or faith fails them. Folklore is full of them because history is full of them — outlaws on the roads, body-snatchers in the churchyards, plague-spreaders real and imagined, and wise women accused of witchcraft for knowing herbs. The dungeon, in this telling, is not only a place where monsters live; it is a place where people go when they have nowhere else to go, or when they want something badly enough to follow it underground.

### Brigand

The brigand is the road outlaw of history given a fantasy name. From the medieval *brigant*, a foot soldier and freebooter, to the highwaymen of early modern England and the bandits of every mountain pass, the brigand is the person who lives by robbing travellers where the road is lonely and the law is thin. Brigands are not an army and not a tribe; they are small groups, often deserters, displaced soldiers, or younger sons with no inheritance, who know the local paths, watch the road from cover, and strike where a traveller is alone and laden. Their stories are the stories of roads themselves: ambushes at crossroads, ransoms, stolen pack animals, and the long, slow work of sheriffs and soldiers trying to clear a pass that fills again as soon as it is emptied.

In folklore the brigand shades into the romantic outlaw — Robin Hood is the famous English example, a brigand made into a hero by ballads — and into the feared stranger, the man at the inn who asks too many questions about your route. The difference between a brigand and a hero in these tales is rarely what they do; it is who they rob and who tells the story. A brigand who robs the rich and spares the poor becomes a legend; a brigand who robs everyone becomes a warning. Both are human, both are armed, and both know the road better than you do.

### Grave Robber

The grave robber is a figure of real history before it is a figure of fantasy. In Britain and America in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, before anatomy laws supplied medical schools with legal cadavers, resurrection men dug up freshly buried bodies and sold them to surgeons for dissection. The trade was so brisk in Edinburgh that Burke and Hare, in 1828, skipped the digging altogether and murdered people to supply the anatomist Robert Knox — a scandal that helped bring about the Anatomy Act of 1832. Grave robbers worked at night, with shovels, sacks, and a knowledge of churchyards, and they were hated not only for theft but for violating the one rest the poor could expect: a quiet grave.

Folklore adds a second layer. The grave robber in tales is not only a thief but a trespasser on sacred ground, and stories punish that trespass: the robber who steals a ring from a corpse finds the corpse following him, the robber who opens the wrong tomb wakes something that was not dead, the robber who disturbs a barrow is cursed by its guardian. The historical grave robber stole bodies for money; the folkloric grave robber steals treasure and finds that the dead do not always stay quiet about it. Both versions share the same tools — shovel, sack, darkness — and the same moral: some ground should not be dug.

### Cultist

The cultist, as a modern figure, is less a single historical person than a composite of religious panic, secret societies, and fantasy fiction. Real history gives us mystery cults of the ancient world, medieval heretical movements, and the secret societies of the early modern period, all of which frightened their neighbours by worshipping in private, using strange rites, and answering to a authority other than church or king. The word *cult* itself, in its modern pejorative sense, is largely a twentieth-century invention, shaped by new religious movements and by the moral panics that surrounded them. The cultist of fantasy takes that fear and gives it a dungeon: a robed figure chanting in a circle, calling on something older and darker than the local gods, and willing to sacrifice anything — including itself — to complete the rite.

What makes the cultist frightening in stories is not strength but conviction. A brigand can be bought off; a cultist, in the tale's telling, cannot, because it believes its god, demon, or dead master will reward it. That fanaticism is also what makes cultists useful villains in fiction: they explain why a dungeon has rituals, circles, and sacrifices in it, and why its inhabitants fight to the death for a cause no outsider can see. The historical record is more ambiguous — most real secret worshippers were simply people whose religion differed from their neighbours' — but the fantasy cultist is not a history lesson. It is the fear of the secret meeting, given robes and a dagger.

### Witch

The witch is one of folklore's oldest and most contested figures, and she refuses to be one thing. In the oldest sense, a witch is a person — usually, though not always, a woman — who works magic outside the sanctioned channels: healing with herbs, cursing with words, flying by night, dealing with spirits, and knowing things she should not know. Slavic folklore gives us Baba Yaga, the forest witch who lives in a hut on chicken legs, flies in a mortar, and tests heroes with impossible tasks, helping those she deems worthy and eating those she does not. Greek myth gives us Medea and Circe, sorceresses whose magic is both help and harm. English and Scottish witch trials of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries give us the darker, persecuted side: ordinary women — healers, midwives, quarrelsome neighbours — accused of malefice, of souring milk, killing cattle, and consorting with familiars, and executed on the strength of accusation and confession.

Modern scholarship stresses how much of the witch is a projection: a community's fears about illness, infertility, bad harvests, and female independence, condensed into a single accused person. Folklore itself is more generous: the witch is also the wise woman, the cunning folk, the healer who knows which herb draws a fever and which root must not be touched. The witch of this bestiary draws on both sides — the herb pouch and the hex, the cottage at the forest's edge and the coven in the dark — because folklore never settled whether the witch was a villain, a victim, or a power in her own right, and it is that unresolved quality that has kept her alive in stories for centuries.

- **Variants:** Baba Yaga of Slavic tales; the cunning woman and wise woman of English folklore; Medea and Circe of Greek myth; the accused witch of the early modern trials.
- **Traits that recur:** herb lore, familiars, flight (broom, mortar, or other domestic vessel), curses and cures in the same hands.

### Berserker

The Berserker, as a human figure, is the Norse *berserkr* described under Orc Berserker, but without the orc: a warrior of the sagas who wore a bear skin — *ber-serkr* means bear-shirt — and fought in the *berserkergang*, a frenzied state in which he was said to feel no pain, no cold, and no fear. The *Ynglinga Saga* and other sources describe berserkers howling, biting their shields, and going into battle naked or in animal skins, and kings kept them as elite shock troops and bodyguards precisely because their frenzy was terrifying to face. Later sagas also show the other side of the bargain: a berserker who could not stop, who turned on friends as readily as enemies, and who was as much a danger to his own side as to the foe.

Historians still argue about what the *berserkergang* was — ritual ecstasy, deliberate psychological warfare, the effect of psychoactive substances, epilepsy, or literary exaggeration — and the sagas themselves, written centuries after the Viking Age, are not reliable field reports. What survives is the image, and it is a powerful one: a human who becomes an animal in battle, not by shapeshifting but by surrendering judgement to rage, and who must be managed, feared, and, when the battle ends, kept away from everyone else until the frenzy passes. The human berserker is that image at its most unsettling, because unlike a troll or an ogre, it is a person who chose this state — or was chosen by it — and who will have to live with what it did.

### Bandit Chief

**thin lore — director call.** There is no traditional figure called the Bandit Chief with a tale cycle of its own. History and folklore give us outlaw leaders — Robin Hood, the German robber captains of the early modern forests, the Balkan *hajduks*, the English highwayman chiefs of ballad — but no single Bandit Chief, no rite of succession, and no custom that distinguishes a chief from any other outlaw captain except that others follow them. The Bandit Chief is a role within brigand lore: the strongest, cleverest, or most charismatic robber, who holds a band together by loot, fear, or loyalty, and whose orders the band obeys. Its lore is brigand lore, told from the front of the band rather than from its edge, and this entry does not invent a separate mythology for it.

### Mad Alchemist

**thin lore — director call.** The Mad Alchemist is a literary and game figure, not a folkloric one. Real alchemy has a long, serious history — from Hellenistic Egypt through the Islamic world to early modern Europe — in which alchemists sought the philosopher's stone, the elixir of life, and the transmutation of base metals into gold, working with furnaces, alembics, and coded texts. Some alchemists were frauds, some were mystics, and some were early chemists, but none is remembered as a dungeon-dwelling thrower of exploding phials. The mad alchemist of fiction takes the real alchemist's workshop — glassware, strange liquids, obsessive secrecy — and adds madness, isolation, and a willingness to test dangerous mixtures on anyone who walks in. Its closest literary kin are figures like the crazed experimenters of Gothic fiction and the obsessive scholars of later horror, not any single alchemical master. No traditional tale gives this creature its own story.

### Necromancer

The necromancer is a figure of medieval learned magic before it is a figure of fantasy dungeons. The word comes from Greek *nekromanteia*, divination by the dead, and medieval necromancy — as described by scholars such as Richard Kieckhefer — was a learned, often clerical art that involved summoning and controlling demons and spirits, frequently by imitating and perverting church ritual. It was not folk magic but book magic: circles, Latin formulae, and long preparations, practised by educated men who believed spirits could be compelled to answer questions or do work. The necromancer's reputation for evil comes from that demonic association, and from the church's condemnation of any traffic with the dead.

Later fantasy kept the book and the circle but changed the job: the modern necromancer does not merely consult the dead, it raises them, commanding skeletons and zombies as servants and soldiers. Tolkien's Necromancer of Dol Guldur — Sauron in disguise — is the bridge: a dark power in a ruined fortress, whose nature is only gradually revealed. The folklore behind the modern version is therefore twofold: the medieval image of the learned magician who deals with spirits, and the much older, wider fear of the unquiet dead, who must be laid to rest rather than called back. A necromancer, in either version, is dangerous because it refuses to let the dead stay dead.

### Warlock

The warlock is a word with a contested history and a modern fantasy life. In older Scots and English usage, *warlock* could mean a male witch, a sorcerer, or, in its oldest sense, an oath-breaker — from Old English *wǣrloga*, one who breaks a covenant — and it was applied to men accused of witchcraft in the same trials that condemned women as witches. In that older sense, a warlock is not a separate species of magician but a male witch, subject to the same accusations, the same familiars, and the same stake. Modern fantasy has reshaped the word into a distinct class: a magic-user who gains power by pact, bargaining with a patron — a demon, a fey lord, an old god, a voice from the deep — and paying for that power with service, obedience, or some part of itself.

That pact is the warlock's real lore. Bargains with devils and spirits are among the oldest stories in the world, from Faust to the crossroads musician, and the warlock is the figure who has made the bargain and now lives with it. The patron's mark, the borrowed power, the price that comes due — these are the warlock's marks in fiction, and they echo the older oath-breaker meaning: a warlock is someone who has sworn to the wrong master, or who has broken the oath that should have bound them. Whether the patron is a demon, a fey court, or something stranger, the story is the same: power now, payment later.

### Plague Bearer

**thin lore — director call.** There is no traditional monster called the Plague Bearer. What history and folklore do give us is a cluster of plague figures, and the Plague Bearer is best understood as a condensation of them. There is the plague doctor of the seventeenth century, in beaked mask and waxed coat, visiting the sick during epidemics. There are the plague-spreaders of accusation — Jews, lepers, beggars, and witches blamed in different times and places for spreading disease deliberately, accusations that led to persecution and murder and that historians now recognise as scapegoating rather than fact. There are the personifications of plague itself, from the biblical Angel of Death to the skeletal Death of the *Danse Macabre*, and the folklore of plague as a spirit, a woman in white, or a breath that moves from house to house. And there are the real carriers of disease: the sick, the fleeing, and the caregivers who carried infection with them unknowingly. No single tale gives us a shambling, diseased figure who spreads sickness as a weapon, and this entry does not invent one; it records the cluster from which the figure is drawn.

### Fallen Knight

The Fallen Knight is a figure of romance and moral tale before it is a figure of dungeons. In Arthurian and chivalric literature, knights fall: they break their oaths, betray their lords, succumb to pride, lust, or despair, and become the enemies of the order they once served. Mordred, who betrays Arthur; Lancelot, whose love for Guinevere fractures the Round Table; the Black Knight of romance, who challenges all comers from a place of disgrace — all are fallen knights in the literary sense, still skilled, still armoured, still bound by a code, but bound now to a darker master or to no master at all. The fallen knight's tragedy is that it remembers what it was: its discipline, its shield-work, and its sense of honour survive its fall, and make it more dangerous than a mere brigand, because it fights like a knight even when it no longer fights for a knight's cause.

Folklore adds a second layer: the ghostly knight, the undead champion who guards a tomb or a bridge and challenges travellers, and the cursed knight who cannot rest until a wrong is righted. The dungeon's fallen knight draws on both: the living traitor who chose a darker path, and the dead champion who cannot leave its post. Either way, the figure's power is its form. Where other human enemies are ragged and desperate, the fallen knight is measured, shielded, and patient — and that patience, learned in a better service, is what makes it frightening now.

# Bestiary lore — Egyptian & Near East, Mesopotamian, South Asian, Chinese & East Asian, Japanese Yōkai

Lore for Abysmal's bestiary, part 6. This is folklore first: what people actually believed, painted, carved, and told stories about, retold in fresh words. Where a creature is really a type, an image, or a guardian figure rather than the hero of a single tale, the entry says so honestly instead of inventing a story for it. No game numbers or mechanics appear here. The companion behavior specs live in `design-notes/behavior-specs/part-6.md`.

## Egyptian & Near East

Egypt's monsters are mostly guardians, judges, and emblems carved into tombs and temples: creatures that stand at a threshold, weigh a soul, or stand in for a god. Many of them have no single adventure of their own — their power is in what they watch over, and in the animals Egyptians already feared or revered along the Nile and in the desert.

### Scarab Swarm

The scarab beetle was one of Egypt's most familiar sacred images. Egyptians watched the dung beetle roll a ball across the sand and saw the sun itself being pushed across the sky, and so the beetle became the sign of Khepri, the morning form of the sun god, and of rebirth. Scarabs were carved as amulets, seals, and ornaments by the thousand, worn by the living and placed with the dead, often inscribed with prayers or a name, and the heart scarab laid on a mummy's chest was meant to keep the heart from testifying against its owner at judgment.

A swarm, then, is less a single monster from a myth than the beetle's sacred power multiplied past comfort: the same creature that promised renewal, arriving in numbers too great to sweep aside. In Egyptian art scarabs appear singly and in profusion on jewelry and funerary goods, and in later storytelling — from ancient tomb-curses in popular imagination to modern adventure tales — the beetle's association with tombs made a crawling mass of them a natural tomb horror. The dread is not one bite but being covered, as a tomb's quiet workforce turns on the intruder.

- **Sacred link:** Khepri, the rising sun; rebirth and protection of the heart.
- **Typical form:** a glossy, iridescent beetle with a broad shell and jointed legs, often shown pushing a round ball.
- **Where it belongs:** tombs, temples, and desert sand — anywhere the dead were meant to rise again.

### Asp

The asp of Egyptian story is the cobra, above all the uraeus cobra worn on the brow of kings and gods. The rearing cobra, hood spread, was the protector of pharaoh: the goddess Wadjet in cobra form spat fire at the king's enemies, and the uraeus on a crown was a living weapon as much as an emblem. Cobras also belonged to the desert edge and to the heat of the land, and a bite from a desert snake was a sudden, ordinary death in a world without antivenom — which is why the snake's strike became a byword for swift, inescapable doom.

In later retelling, the asp is inseparable from Cleopatra, said to have died by the bite of an asp smuggled into her chambers in a basket of figs, though ancient writers themselves disagreed about exactly how she died and the story may be as much political legend as fact. What matters for the bestiary is the older Egyptian picture: a small, proud, coiled creature that does not chase, but rears, spreads its hood, and kills with one precise strike when its ground is crossed.

- **Sacred link:** Wadjet, protector goddess; the uraeus on the royal crown.
- **Defining traits:** rearing posture, flared hood, a strike that comes with almost no warning beyond the display itself.
- **Variant:** the Egyptian cobra of the Nile valley and the smaller desert vipers and horned vipers that share its reputation.

### Jackal Guardian

Jackals haunted Egypt's desert cemeteries, scavenging at the edges of burial grounds, and Egyptians turned that uneasy fact into a guardian. Anubis, the jackal-headed god, watched over embalming, guided the dead, and stood beside the scales at judgment; Wepwawet, the "opener of the ways," also took jackal form and went ahead of armies and funeral processions. A jackal at a tomb door was therefore not simply a scavenger — it was the animal shape of the god who kept the dead safe and kept robbers out.

The guardian in the bestiary draws on that whole tradition rather than on one named god. Jackal-headed figures appear in funerary paintings, on coffins, and as statues set to watch over burial chambers, and the jackal's real habits — circling, darting in, retreating, working in loose packs at the desert's edge — gave artists a ready picture of a watcher that never quite lets its prey out of sight. It is a creature of thresholds: the doorway between the living world and the burial ground, where it decides who may pass and who is prey.

- **Sacred links:** Anubis, god of embalming and the dead; Wepwawet, opener of the ways.
- **Defining traits:** black or dark-coated jackal form, keen circling movement, loyalty to a threshold rather than open pursuit.
- **Note:** not every jackal figure is Anubis himself — many are his attendants, statues, or lesser guardians in his shape.

### Guardian Scorpion

*thin lore — director call*

Scorpions were a real and daily danger in Egypt, and the Egyptians gave them a goddess: Serqet (Selkis), who wore a scorpion on her head and was invoked both against stings and as a protector of the dead, guarding the throne and the canopic jars that held the viscera. Magical texts and amulets called on Serqet to neutralize venom, and scorpion figures were carved as protective charms — a creature too dangerous to ignore, pressed into service as a warden. Temple and tomb guardians in scorpion form belong to that same logic: set a feared thing at the door, and lesser threats will think twice.

There is, however, no single named "guardian scorpion" hero or villain in Egyptian myth with its own story, and this entry does not invent one. What exists is the animal, the goddess, the sting, and the protective magic around them, plus the long Egyptian habit of carving dangerous animals as apotropaic guardians. A guardian scorpion in Abysmal is therefore best read as a temple warden in that tradition — a carved or consecrated scorpion set to hold a threshold — rather than as a figure from a lost tale.

- **Sacred link:** Serqet, scorpion goddess, healer of stings and protector of the dead.
- **Defining traits:** raised tail, raised claws, a sting that is the whole point of the creature.
- **What is not claimed:** no individual guardian scorpion with a name and a myth survives in the sources.

### Serpopard

*thin lore — director call*

The serpopard is a creature known only from pictures, and its very name is modern — a joining of "serpent" and "leopard" coined by scholars because no ancient text gives the animal a name at all. It appears on decorated cosmetic palettes from Predynastic Egypt, most famously the Narmer Palette, where two of the beasts stand with their long necks intertwined, and on cylinder seals from Protoliterate Mesopotamia around 3500–3000 BCE, often in symmetrical pairs. The body is feline — scholars debate leopard versus lioness, noting the round ears and tufted tail — while the neck stretches out long and serpentine, questing ahead of the body.

Because there is no story attached to it, interpretations are scholarly rather than mythic. In Egyptian art the serpopard has been read as an emblem of the chaos beyond Egypt's borders, a wild thing the king must tame or hold in check — on the Narmer Palette the intertwined pair are held by attendants, as if leashed. In Mesopotamia the paired, intertwined beasts have been read as rivers, as the meeting of lands, or as a manifestation of the wild vitality breaking out of the earth. It may also be a misreading of something else entirely: some scholars suggest giraffe bones or a stylized giraffe behind the long neck. What survives is the image — a predator whose reach is longer than its body — and that image is the whole of its lore.

- **Known from:** Narmer Palette, Oxford (Two Dog) Palette, Uruk cylinder seals.
- **Defining traits:** feline body, extremely long neck, often shown in a pair with necks intertwined.
- **What is not claimed:** no ancient name, no myth, and no single agreed meaning.

### Sha Beast

*thin lore — director call*

The sha is the animal of the god Set (Seth), and like the serpopard it is known from images rather than from a tale of its own. In Egyptian art the sha, also called the Set animal or Typhonic beast, is a slender canid — something between a greyhound, a jackal, and a fennec fox — with three features that never vary: tall ears that are narrow at the base and squared off at the top, a long snout that curves slightly downward, and a stiff tail, often forked at the end, that stands upright or at an angle whether the animal sits, stands, or walks. It appears as a hieroglyph, as a determinative marking words to do with disorder and violence, on the Scorpion Macehead, on the serekhs of Second Dynasty kings, and as the head of Set himself in his familiar human-bodied, animal-headed form.

Egyptologists have proposed a dozen real animals behind it — aardvark, donkey, giraffe, okapi, saluki, African wild dog — and none fits every feature, so the current consensus is that the sha was a composite or wholly imaginary beast, invented to embody Set's own nature: god of deserts, storms, disorder, and foreigners, the murderer of Osiris and the rival of Horus. It has, as one standard summary puts it, no independent mythology outside its association with Set. Its menace is therefore borrowed and total: wherever the sha appears, Set's storm and strangeness are present with it.

- **Also called:** Set animal, Typhonic beast.
- **Defining traits:** squared-topped erect ears, down-curved snout, stiff forked upright tail, slender canine body.
- **What is not claimed:** no story in which the sha acts alone, apart from Set.

### Falcon Sphinx

*thin lore — director call*

Egypt's most famous guardian is the sphinx: a lion's body with a human head, as at Giza, guarding a temple approach or a royal tomb. But Egyptian art also knew the hieracosphinx, a sphinx with the head of a falcon, and the criosphinx, with a ram's head — variants that lined temple avenues, especially at Karnak, where rows of ram-headed sphinxes still stand. The falcon head points to Horus, the sky god whose eye was the sun and moon, and to the king as Horus incarnate: a falcon-headed guardian is a royal, sky-borne watcher, swifter and more keen-eyed than the human-headed kind.

Like the serpopard, the falcon sphinx has iconography rather than narrative. No surviving tale sends a hieracosphinx on an adventure or gives it riddles to ask, as the Greek sphinx later acquired. Its meaning lives in placement: set in rows along a processional way, or at a doorway, it announces that the ground beyond is sacred and watched, and that the watcher sees from above as a falcon does. The lion's body supplies the strength to hold that ground; the falcon's head supplies the sight to judge who approaches it.

- **Related forms:** androsphinx (human head), criosphinx (ram head), hieracosphinx (falcon head).
- **Sacred link:** Horus, falcon god of kingship and the sky.
- **What is not claimed:** no individual falcon sphinx with a name or a story.

### Devourer Beast

Egypt's Devourer is Ammit, whose Egyptian name means "Devourer of the Dead" — also rendered Ammut or Ahemait. She waited beside the scales in the Hall of Two Truths, in the underworld Duat, while Anubis weighed the heart of the deceased against the feather of Ma'at, goddess of truth. If the heart proved heavy with wrongdoing, Ammit ate it, and the soul was left restless and barred from Osiris and from the blessed afterlife. She was not worshipped — Egyptians feared her rather than prayed to her — because she was the consequence of failing judgment, not a goddess who could be bargained with.

Her body was deliberately assembled from the three most feared man-eaters Egyptians knew: the head of a crocodile, the forequarters of a lion or lioness, and the hindquarters of a hippopotamus. Each part was a real Nile danger — the crocodile's ambush, the lion's strength, the hippo's bulk and aggression — and together they said that no one, in life or after it, could outrun being judged. She appears in the Book of the Dead and in tomb paintings such as the Papyrus of Ani, crouched by the scales, mouth open, waiting for the verdict to go wrong.

- **Also called:** Ammit, Ammut, Ahemait; "Eater of Hearts," "Great of Death."
- **Form:** crocodile head, lion forequarters, hippopotamus hindquarters.
- **Role:** not a hunter of the living, but the end of the road for the judged and found wanting.

### Ushabti

The ushabti — also spelled shabti or shawabti — was an Egyptian funerary figurine, and one of the most numerous antiquities ever made. Placed in tombs among the grave goods, ushabtis were meant to act as servants or substitutes for the deceased in the afterlife: when the dead were called upon to do manual labor in the Field of Reeds — to plough, to sow, to carry sand, to fill channels with water — the figurine would answer in their place. The later name itself is often explained as "answerer," because the figure answers the summons to work. Many carry Spell 6 of the Book of the Dead, inscribed on their legs, in which the figure declares itself ready to do whatever work is decreed.

The custom grew over two thousand years. Early figures represented the deceased themselves; by the New Kingdom they had become a workforce, and an elite tomb was ideally supplied with 401 of them — one for each day of the year, plus 36 overseers, one for each ten-day week of the Egyptian calendar. They were made of faience, stone, wood, clay, or metal, usually mummiform with arms crossed over the chest, holding a hoe and a basket or a pick, and the tomb of Tutankhamun alone held more than four hundred. They are, in short, a whole labor force in miniature, patient, identical, and endlessly replaceable — which is exactly why a tomb full of them, waking at once, is a chilling thought.

- **Abysmal take:** in folklore ushabtis work in the afterlife; in Abysmal they march and strike with their tools. The rank, the hoe, and the answering call are the folklore; the hostility is the game's.
- **Also called:** shabti (earlier term), shawabti (Theban variant).
- **Typical form:** small mummiform figure, arms crossed, hoe on the shoulder, basket on the back, inscription on the legs.

## Mesopotamian

Mesopotamia's monsters guard gates, enforce the underworld's bargains, and embody the wild country beyond the irrigated plain. Its stories are older than most of the world's literature — the Epic of Gilgamesh, the Descent of Inanna, the Exploits of Ninurta — and its creatures tend to be hybrids carved at doorways: bull, lion, eagle, and serpent combined into a single sentinel, or demons whose job is simply never to let go.

### Cedar Guardian

*thin lore — director call*

The Cedar Forest in the Epic of Gilgamesh is a sacred, distant wood of towering cedars, guarded by a terrifying keeper whose permission even kings must respect. Gilgamesh and his friend Enkidu journey there to cut cedar and win fame, and they must face that guardian — but the guardian himself is the apex Humbaba, whose lore is kept separately, and this bestiary entry is for the lesser Cedar Guardian, the forest's watchers in his shadow. As a named individual, the lesser guardian has no story of its own; what the epic gives us is the forest itself: a place of resin-scented darkness, of cedar so tall it brushes heaven, set aside for the gods and forbidden to ordinary cutting.

That is the lore this creature carries: the sacred grove, the rule that a forest belonging to the gods needs a keeper, and the terror of a being that is half tree and half watchman, rooted in its ground and unwilling to leave it. Cedar was precious in treeless Mesopotamia — imported at great cost for temples and palaces — so a guardian of cedar guarded real wealth as well as sacred ground, and cutting it without leave was both theft and sacrilege. The guardian's patience is the forest's patience: it does not hunt, it waits where the trees are, and it objects when the axe arrives.

- **Apex note:** the apex Humbaba is a separate entry, covered in the apex lore; this entry is the lesser forest watcher, not Humbaba himself.
- **Source story:** the Epic of Gilgamesh, the journey to the Cedar Forest.
- **Defining traits:** massive, tree-like bulk, resin and cedar scent, rooted to its grove ground.

### Galla

The galla — also spelled gallû or gallu — are the demons of the Mesopotamian underworld, and they are best known from the Descent of Inanna, one of the oldest descent-into-death stories in the world. When Inanna, queen of heaven, returns from the land of the dead, the law of the underworld demands a substitute, and the galla escort her back to the world above to claim one. The poem describes them with grim precision: they know no food, no drink, no flour offering, no libation; they never enjoy marriage or children; they snatch the son from a man's knee and tear the bride from her father-in-law's house. They try to take Inanna's faithful servant Ninshubur, then her sons, then her household — and Inanna refuses each time, because each of them is dressed in mourning for her. Only when she finds her husband Dumuzi sitting on his throne in fine robes, not mourning at all, does she turn the eye of death on him and hand him to the galla, who seize him, beat him, and drag him below.

There is a second, stranger face to the galla in the same poem. When Inanna lies dead, hung on a hook in Ereshkigal's throne room, the god Enki fashions two small creatures from the dirt under his fingernails — the gala-turra and kur-jara in some tellings, themselves a kind of galla — neither male nor female, who slip into the underworld like flies, sympathize with Ereshkigal in her labor pains, and are rewarded with Inanna's corpse, which they revive with the food and water of life. The same word thus covers both the pitiless bailiffs and the tiny sympathizers: beings outside ordinary human life, who can enter where the living cannot and who enforce bargains without mercy or rest.

- **Also called:** gallû, gallu; Akkadian form of Sumerian gal.lu.
- **Source story:** the Descent of Inanna (early second millennium BCE).
- **Defining traits:** tireless pursuit, no appetite for food or pleasure, a grip that drags its victim toward the underworld.

### Scorpion Man

The scorpion men — Akkadian aqrabuamelu — stand at the ends of the earth in the Epic of Gilgamesh, guarding the twin-peaked mountain of Mashu, where the sun rises and sets. They are human above and scorpion below, or human in torso with a scorpion's tail and claws, and their glance alone is said to be death: the poem says that looking at them kills, and that their splendor is terrifying. When Gilgamesh comes to the mountain on his search for immortality, the scorpion man's wife first warns that two-thirds of Gilgamesh is divine, and her husband then questions him, hears his quest, and — moved, or bound by the law of guests — lets him pass into the dark road through the mountain toward the waters of death and the tavern-keeper Siduri.

Scorpion men also appear in Babylonian art as protective figures and in astrological lore, where the scorpion's sting already belonged to the dangerous edge of the world. They are not villains in Gilgamesh so much as a test: a threshold that kills the unworthy by sight alone, but that can be reasoned with by a hero who states his purpose plainly. Their domain is the pass, the mountain gate, the place where the known world ends and the road to the sun's rising begins, and they hold it with bow and sting together.

- **Also called:** aqrabuamelu, scorpion-man.
- **Source story:** the Epic of Gilgamesh, the mountain of Mashu.
- **Defining traits:** human upper body, scorpion tail and claws, a gaze said to bring death, a guardian's post at a mountain pass.

### Ugallu

The ugallu, also called the "great storm-beast" or "big weather-beast," is one of Mesopotamia's lion-demons: a creature with a lion's body and head and the legs and talons of a great bird of prey, often shown holding a dagger and a mace. It belongs to the company of protective demons carved on palace walls and worn as amulets — beings whose ferocity was turned outward, against disease and evil spirits, the way a guard dog's ferocity is turned against intruders. Ugallu figures appear in Neo-Assyrian art as apotropaic guardians, and the creature's storm association ties it to the violent weather that broke over the Mesopotamian plain: sudden, loud, and impossible to argue with.

In the older lists of demons, the ugallu stands alongside other lion-headed and bird-footed hybrids whose job was to frighten away what frightened people. It is not the hero of a single epic — no poem follows an ugallu on a quest — but its iconography is consistent: leonine strength, raptor feet, weapons in hand, a creature built for the charge. Later tradition sometimes conflated it with the lion-headed demon of disease and the underworld, but in its palace-guardian form it is best understood as a storm made into a sentinel, set at a door to meet force with force.

- **Also called:** great storm-beast; sometimes grouped with the lion-demons of Assyrian protective art.
- **Defining traits:** lion head and body, bird's legs and talons, dagger and mace, storm-driven charge.
- **Role:** apotropaic guardian — a fierce thing set to frighten off fiercer things.

### Lamassu

The lamassu — female form lamassu, male form shedu in later usage — is the great winged guardian of Assyrian palaces and city gates: a bull's body, an eagle's wings, and a human head, often bearded and wearing a horned cap, carved in colossal stone at doorways. Anyone who has seen the great gate figures from Khorsabad or Nimrud, now in museums in Paris, London, and Chicago, has met a lamassu: five legs, so that it appears to stand firm from the front and to stride forward from the side, a piece of sculptural cunning that makes the guardian both at rest and in motion at once.

The lamassu was a protective spirit, a benevolent power set to turn away evil and to guard the king's house. It combined the strongest, swiftest, and wisest creatures known — bull for strength, eagle for speed and height, human for intelligence — into a single sentinel whose message was plain: this threshold is watched by something that cannot be outrun, outfought, or outwitted. Unlike the demons of the underworld, the lamassu was on the side of the household it guarded, and its presence at a gate was a blessing as much as a warning. The name itself carried the sense of a protective genius, a personal guardian spirit grown to palace scale.

- **Forms:** lamassu (often female in earlier usage), shedu (male); winged bull or winged lion variants.
- **Defining traits:** bull's body, eagle's wings, human head, five legs in the great gate sculptures.
- **Where it stands:** palace and city gates, especially Neo-Assyrian Khorsabad and Nimrud.

### Mušḫuššu

The mušḫuššu — the name means roughly "furious snake" or "splendid serpent" — is the dragon of Babylon, best known from the Ishtar Gate, where its image marched in glazed brick alongside bulls and lions. It has a serpent's long, scaled body and neck, a lion's forelegs, an eagle's hind talons, a forked tongue, and a crest or horns, and it was the sacred animal and attendant of Marduk, chief god of Babylon, who is shown standing on it or holding its leash in art. Its earlier history ties it to the god Ninazu and to underworld powers, but by the Neo-Babylonian period it was unmistakably Marduk's beast: the dragon that belonged to the god who had defeated chaos.

In the creation epic Enūma Eliš, Marduk defeats the chaos dragon Tiamat, and the mušḫuššu has sometimes been read in that light — a tamed chaos-serpent, now serving order instead of threatening it. On the Ishtar Gate it is not a monster to be slain but a heraldic beast, repeated in procession, announcing whose city this is. Its mixed anatomy is the point: serpent for the deep and the earth, lion for strength, eagle for the sky, all bound into one creature that answers to a single god.

- **Also called:** mushhushshu, sirrush (an older popular rendering).
- **Sacred link:** Marduk of Babylon; earlier, Ninazu.
- **Defining traits:** serpent body and neck, lion forelegs, eagle hind legs, forked tongue, crest.
- **Where it appears:** the Ishtar Gate of Babylon, and Marduk's iconography generally.

### Anzu

Anzu — also Anzû or Imdugud in its earlier Sumerian form — is the great storm bird of Mesopotamia: a massive eagle or lion-headed eagle whose wings beat up the storm, who steals the Tablet of Destinies, and whose defeat is the subject of an epic. In the Sumerian telling, Imdugud is the thundercloud bird, the roaring storm itself with a lion's head; in the later Akkadian Anzu Epic, Anzu is the guardian of Enlil's sanctuary who, while bathing Enlil, steals the Tablet of Destinies that decrees the fates of gods and men, and with it seizes Enlil's power. The gods send champion after champion — Adad, Girra, Shara — and each refuses, until the warrior god Ninurta takes up the fight, loses his weapons to Anzu's storm, and finally, with the counsel of Enki and his talking mace Sharur, brings the bird down and recovers the tablet.

Anzu is thus both a natural force and a cautionary thief: the storm that serves the gods but overreaches, the servant who mistakes custody for ownership. Its image — lion-headed eagle, wings spread over mountains, talons like a lion's — appears in art from the third millennium onward, sometimes as a benevolent emblem of a city or god and sometimes as the monster whose fall restores order. The bird's scream in the epics is the thunder, its wing-beats the wind, and its fall is the storm breaking.

- **Apex note:** the apex Anzu is a separate entry, covered in the apex lore; this entry is the lesser storm bird, not the Tablet-thief of the epic in his full power.
- **Also called:** Anzû, Imdugud (Sumerian).
- **Source stories:** the Anzu Epic; Sumerian Lugalbanda tales.
- **Defining traits:** lion-headed eagle, vast wings, storm voice, thief of the Tablet of Destinies.

### Asag

Asag — Sumerian a₂-sag₃, Akkadian asakku — is the monstrous demon of the Sumerian poem Lugal-e, the Exploits of Ninurta, and one of Mesopotamia's great images of landscape turned hostile. He is the offspring of An, the sky, and Ki, the earth, a hideous being whose power is so foul that fish boil alive in the rivers at his approach. He mates with the mountains themselves and raises an army of stone offspring — personified rocks and boulders that rise as warriors — and with them he blocks the waters, threatens the plain, and unmakes the ordered world the gods have built.

Ninurta, son of Enlil and god of war and agriculture, takes counsel from his father and goes out against Asag with Sharur, his enchanted talking mace, which flies ahead, scouts, and advises him. The battle is long and the poem dwells on its scale: rivers steaming, mountains shaking, stones rising. When Ninurta wins, he does not merely kill the monster — he judges the stones, assigning good destinies to the loyal ones and bad to the rebels, and then reshapes the landscape, building the foothill Hursag and cutting channels so the Tigris can water the plain. Asag's name also lived on as a class of disease demons, the asakku, blamed for fevers and afflictions that seemed to come from nowhere, which is why the demon of Lugal-e feels like both a single monster and a spreading sickness: the ground itself gone wrong.

- **Also called:** Azag; Akkadian asakku (also a class of fever demons).
- **Source story:** Lugal-e, the Exploits of Ninurta.
- **Defining traits:** hideous, mountain-born, attended by an army of living stones; his presence fouls water and spreads disease.
- **Fate:** slain by Ninurta, whose victory reorders mountains and rivers.

## South Asian

South Asian lore is vast and layered — Vedic hymns, the epics Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa, Buddhist and Jain tales, and living folk and temple traditions all feed it — and its creatures refuse simple sorting into good and evil. A nāga can be a guardian or a flood; a yaksha can bless a grove or haunt it; an asura can be a proud rival of the gods rather than a devil. The bestiary entries below follow that moral complexity, not a simple monster manual.

### Naga

The nāga are serpent beings of Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain tradition: part human and part serpent, or great multi-headed cobras with hoods spread, who live in the underworld waters of Pātāla and in rivers, lakes, and wells. They guard treasure, water, and sacred places, and they are both feared and honored — a nāga angered can bring drought or flood, while a nāga honored brings rain and fertility. They are not mere animals: in the epics they have kings, palaces, and lineages. Vāsuki, king of the serpents, served as the churning rope when gods and demons churned the ocean for the nectar of immortality, and the great serpent Śeṣa holds the world on his hoods, with the god Vishnu resting upon him between ages of the world.

In Buddhist story the nāga Mucalinda spread his hoods over the Buddha to shelter him from a storm during his meditation, and nāgas appear as converts, protectors of relics, and keepers of hidden teachings — the Mahāyāna tradition says nāgas guarded the Prajñāpāramitā sutras beneath the sea until humanity was ready for them. Regionally, nāgas shade from the multi-headed cobra of Indian temple carving to the serpent-dragons of Southeast Asian rivers, where they guard temple stairways and causeways. The defining image stays constant: the raised hood, the coil, the water at the serpent's back, and a being that is dangerous to cross and wise to honor.

- **Major figures:** Śeṣa (world-serpent), Vāsuki (churning rope), Mucalinda (the Buddha's shelter).
- **Defining traits:** human-serpent form or multi-headed cobra, raised hood, coils, dominion over water and treasure.
- **Temperament:** guardian and threat at once — appeased by offerings, angered by pollution or insult to water.

### Yaksha

Yakshas — feminine yakshi or yakshini — are nature spirits of Indian tradition, guardians of groves, springs, lakes, and buried treasure, attached to the god of wealth Kubera, whose own attendants they are. In early art they are powerful, broad, sensuous figures, carved on gateways and railings at Buddhist stupas such as Bharhut and Sanchi, where a yakshi grasping a tree branch is one of the most recognizable images in Indian sculpture: fertility, abundance, and the living power of a place, made into a guardian. A yaksha who is honored guards the wealth of its grove; a yaksha who is neglected or offended can turn mischievous or hostile, and folk tales are full of yakshas who test travelers, demand offerings, or punish greed.

The Yaksha Praśna, the "Questions of the Yaksha," in the Mahābhārata gives the creature its most famous literary moment: a yaksha who owns a lake forbids the Pāṇḍava brothers to drink until they answer his questions, and kills each brother who drinks without answering, until Yudhiṣṭhira answers rightly and wins his brothers back. The story captures the yaksha's real character better than any bestiary label: not a mindless brute but a keeper with a claim, a question, and a price, whose violence is the enforcement of a boundary. In Jain and Buddhist art yakshas continue as attendants and protectors, and in Sri Lankan and Southeast Asian tradition they shade into the yakku and related spirits of place.

- **Sacred links:** Kubera, god of wealth; guardians of trees, water, and treasure.
- **Defining traits:** powerful, broad-bodied guardian figure, often shown with a tree, a club, or a purse of wealth.
- **Source story:** the Yaksha Praśna in the Mahābhārata.

### Bhūta

Bhūtas are the restless dead of Indian folk belief: ghosts of those who died violently, untimely, or without proper rites, who linger as spirits rather than passing on. The word bhūta itself means "that which has been" — a being, a past thing — and in village tradition bhūtas haunt cremation grounds, abandoned houses, and lonely trees, causing illness, bad dreams, and misfortune, and are dealt with by exorcists, offerings, and protective rites rather than by weapons. They are not a single monster with a biography but a whole class of the unquiet dead, and their stories vary by region: in some places a bhūta is a shadow that follows a traveler home; in others it is a spirit that possesses the living or demands food and recognition.

Classical Sanskrit literature uses bhūta more broadly for spirits, elements, and living beings, and the folk bhūta overlaps with related beings — piśācas, vetālas, pretas — in a crowded underworld of hungry ghosts. The horror of the bhūta is not its shape, which is often barely described, but its wrongness: a person who should have moved on and has not, drifting where the living walk, drawn to grief, death, and the places where rites were left undone. Offerings at crossroads, the lighting of lamps, and the performance of the missing funeral rites are the traditional answers — the living settle the debt the dead could not.

- **Meaning:** bhūta, "that which has been"; a ghost, especially of the untimely or improperly buried dead.
- **Haunts:** cremation grounds, deserted houses, lonely trees, places of grief.
- **Related beings:** piśāca, vetāla, preta — the hungry and restless dead of Indian lore.

### Piśāca

The piśācas are flesh-eating demons of Indian tradition, counted among the lowest and most feared of spirit beings. Vedic and epic texts name them as eaters of raw flesh who haunt cremation grounds and battlefields, and later literature makes them the demons of madness and disease, whispering to the unbalanced and feeding on the dead. They are described as dark, gaunt, and misshapen, with protruding veins and staring eyes, moving in the shadows where corpses lie, and their name became a byword for cruelty and corruption: a piśāca is not merely a monster but a being that has given itself wholly to appetite.

Unlike the yaksha, who guards a place and can be honored, or the nāga, who can be bargained with, the piśāca in most tellings has no bargain to offer. It is drawn to carrion, to the wounded, and to the alone, and it flees from firm resistance and from sacred sound — mantras, bells, and the presence of the gods are its traditional repellents. The Piśāca language, Paisaci, was also named for them in ancient Indian linguistics, said to be the tongue of these demons and, by tradition, the original language of the great story collection Brihatkatha. The creature's legacy is therefore double: a ghoul in the graveyard, and a name for everything that feeds on what should be left in peace.

- **Nature:** flesh-eating ghoul-demons of cremation grounds and battlefields.
- **Defining traits:** gaunt, dark, carrion-quick, drawn to the wounded and the dead, repelled by sacred sound and firm resistance.
- **Cultural echo:** Paisaci, the "language of the piśācas," in Indian literary tradition.

### Rakshasa

The rākṣasas are the great shapeshifting demons of the Indian epics, most famously of the Rāmāyaṇa, where their king Rāvaṇa — ten-headed, twenty-armed, a scholar and a tyrant at once — abducts Sītā and brings Rāma's war upon Laṅkā. Rākṣasas in the epics are not mere brutes: they are powerful, often learned, masters of illusion (māyā) who can take any form, grow or shrink at will, and command armies, and their evil lies in the misuse of that power — in devouring sages, disrupting sacrifices, and seizing what is not theirs. Earlier Vedic texts already name rakshas as night-wanderers who must be warded off by fire and prayer, and the epics develop them into a whole people with cities, kings, and rivalries, some of whom — like Vibhīṣaṇa, Rāvaṇa's brother — choose righteousness and defect to Rāma's side.

That complexity is the point. A rākṣasa can be a terrifying illusionist who appears as a golden deer to lure Rāma away from Sītā, as Mārīca does, or a loyal brother who counsels surrender, or a forest demon like Hidimba who falls in love with a hero. Their shapeshifting is not a trick added for flavor but their nature: beings whose true form is power itself, wearing whatever shape serves the moment. In art they are shown as large, fanged, fierce figures, sometimes with animal features, and in temple tradition they are the forces the gods and heroes must repeatedly subdue — and sometimes, as with Vibhīṣaṇa, the enemy who proves that even a rākṣasa can choose otherwise.

- **Apex note:** the apex Rāvaṇa is a separate entry, covered in the apex lore; this entry is the rākṣasa kind, not their ten-headed king.
- **Source story:** the Rāmāyaṇa above all; also the Mahābhārata and Vedic hymns.
- **Defining traits:** great strength, shapeshifting and illusion, fanged and fierce form, a people with kings and cities, not a lone monster.

### Makara

The makara is the composite water-beast of Indian art: crocodile jaws, an elephant's trunk or snout in many renderings, a fish's tail, and sometimes a lion's paws — a creature assembled from the most powerful water and land animals, set at the thresholds of temples and the ends of stairways. It is the mount (vāhana) of Varuṇa, god of the waters, and of the river goddess Gaṅgā, and it appears as a torana ornament, a spout, and a guardian on temple gateways across India and Southeast Asia, where it shades into the dragon-like makara of Khmer and Thai art. Its composite form is deliberate: the makara is the water itself, in all its aspects — the crocodile's ambush, the elephant's bulk, the fish's speed — made into a single guardian of the boundary between land and river.

In Hindu cosmology the makara also gives its name to Capricorn, the zodiac sign, and to the crocodile-like vehicle of love's god Kāma in some traditions. It is not a villain of epic but a heraldic and architectural beast, and its menace is that of the river: a jaw that closes on whatever enters the water, a bulk that cannot be shifted, a guardian that belongs to the threshold it keeps and will not leave it for the dry land. Where a makara appears in art, it usually appears in pairs, flanking a doorway or disgorging a line of smaller creatures from its open mouth — the river giving forth life even as it threatens to swallow it.

- **Sacred links:** vāhana of Varuṇa and Gaṅgā; zodiac sign Makara (Capricorn).
- **Defining traits:** crocodile jaws, elephant-like snout, fish tail, sometimes lion paws; shown at temple thresholds and stairways.
- **Role:** water guardian — the river's power, carved into a sentinel.

### Asura

The asuras are the great rivals of the devas, the gods, in Indian tradition — and their story is one of the tradition's most interesting reversals. In the oldest Vedic hymns, asura is a title of power that can belong to gods themselves, including Varuṇa; only gradually, in later Vedic and epic literature, do the asuras harden into the demons and titans who war against the devas for dominion of the world. They are not weak or bestial: they are proud, powerful, often ascetic and learned, and they win boons from the gods by terrible austerities, then use those boons to seize heaven — until a god, often Vishnu in an avatar, outwits them and restores the balance. The demon king Bali, the buffalo demon Mahiṣa, and the brothers Śumbha and Niśumbha are all asuras, and their defeats are among Hinduism's great festival stories.

What defines an asura, then, is not a shape — they can be human-formed, multi-armed, or monstrous — but a stance: power seized and held against the cosmic order, pride that refuses to yield, and a challenge issued openly to the gods. They build cities, field armies, and keep courts, and their wars with the devas are cosmic politics rather than mere monster hunts. In Buddhist tradition the asuras become one of the six realms of rebirth: beings of great power and constant envy, forever fighting the gods above them and never content — a moral condition, not just a species. That is why an asura in the bestiary should feel like a warrior-aristocrat of the other side: deliberate, proud, and dangerous precisely because it believes its cause is just.

- **Meaning:** asura, a powerful being; in later tradition, a rival of the devas.
- **Major figures:** Bali, Mahiṣāsura, Śumbha and Niśumbha, Vṛtra in his earlier form.
- **Defining traits:** great power and pride, mastery of austerity and boons, open challenge rather than ambush, a court and army behind the individual.

### Garuda

Garuda is the great eagle of Indian tradition, king of birds, mount of Vishnu, and the eternal enemy of the nāgas. His story is told in the Mahābhārata's Āstīka Parva: born to Vinatā, who was enslaved by her co-wife Kadrū, mother of the serpents, Garuda wins his mother's freedom by stealing the nectar of immortality (amṛta) from the gods, defeating Indra's hosts on the way, and then — refusing to drink it himself — bargains it to the serpents for Vinatā's release, while Indra steals it back before they can drink. For his self-restraint Vishnu takes him as his mount, and Garuda becomes the emblem of courage, speed, and selfless service: the bird who could have seized immortality and chose his mother's freedom instead.

In art Garuda is a vast eagle, often shown with a human body and eagle's wings, beak, and talons, golden and sun-bright, carrying Vishnu on his back or tearing serpents in his claws. His enmity with the nāgas is the order of nature in mythic form — eagle against serpent — and he is invoked as a protector against snakebite and poison, his image worn as an amulet and carved on temples across India, Thailand (where he is the national emblem), and Indonesia. He is not a wild monster but a noble adversary: swift, proud, and loyal, whose stoop from the sky is the arrival of a god's own champion.

- **Sacred link:** vāhana (mount) of Vishnu; enemy and counterweight of the nāgas.
- **Source story:** the Mahābhārata, Āstīka Parva — the theft of amṛta for his mother's freedom.
- **Defining traits:** vast golden eagle, human-eagle hybrid in much art, sun-bright wings, serpent-slayer, loyal mount rather than free predator.

## Chinese & East Asian

Chinese lore sorts its creatures by moral office as much as by shape: a judge with one horn, a glutton with a face and no body, a chaos with no face at all, a fox that has lived long enough to become something else. These are not a random monster list but a cosmology in animal form, drawn from the Classic of Mountains and Seas (Shanhaijing), from imperial symbolism, and from centuries of folk tale — and several of them arrive in Abysmal already changed by the game's own hand.

### Terracotta Soldier

The Terracotta Army is not a myth but a discovery: in 1974, farmers digging a well near Xi'an uncovered pits holding thousands of life-sized clay soldiers, horses, and chariots, buried to guard the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor of China, who unified China in 221 BCE and died in 210 BCE. The army was meant to protect the emperor in the afterlife as it had in life: infantry, archers, charioteers, and generals, each figure modeled with individual features, hairstyles, and armor, originally painted in bright colors, standing in battle formation in long corridors beneath the earth. Estimates run to more than eight thousand figures, with hundreds of horses and dozens of chariots, and excavation continues — the emperor's own burial mound remains unopened.

That a guard force should be made of clay rather than men is the point: the First Emperor's tomb is a whole underground empire, with rivers of mercury, a map of the heavens, and an army that would never desert, never age, and never need feeding. The soldiers' silence is their menace in story after story since the discovery: a drilled rank that has waited two thousand years without moving, and that — in fiction, and in Abysmal — does not need to wait any longer. The historical army never marched; its power was symbolic, a statement that the emperor's rule extended even into death. The game's marching rank takes that statement literally.

- **Abysmal take:** in history the army stands in formation and never moves; in Abysmal it marches. The drilled rank, the clay armor, and the tomb ground are the history; the animation is the game's.
- **Historical facts:** tomb of Qin Shi Huang near Xi'an; discovered 1974; 8,000+ figures; originally painted.
- **Typical form:** life-sized clay soldier in Qin armor, spear or halberd in hand, individualized face, standing in rank.

### Mountain Yaoguai

Yaoguai is a general Chinese term, not a single creature: yao means something strange, uncanny, or demonic, and guai means monster or spirit, so a yaoguai is any being that has gained supernatural power outside the proper order — an animal that has cultivated itself into a demon, a plant or stone that has awakened, a spirit that preys on travelers. The great novel Journey to the West is crowded with them: mountain demons who block the pilgrims' road, who have studied the Way just enough to become dangerous, who keep lairs, command lesser demons, and try to eat the monk Xuanzang because eating his flesh is said to grant immortality. Each has a name, a mountain, and a trick; the category is the point, not any one member.

A "mountain yaoguai," then, is a yaoguai whose ground is the mountain path — the bandit-demon of the high road, the beast that has lived long enough on a peak to learn speech and appetite. Chinese folk tradition is full of such figures: tiger demons, wolf demons, and nameless mountain sprites who lure travelers off the path, and the Classic of Mountains and Seas catalogues mountain beings by the hundreds, each tied to a particular peak and its dangers. This entry therefore does not pretend that one named mountain yaoguai with a single story exists. What exists is the type — the cultivated beast of the mountain road, strong, territorial, and hungry — and the long Chinese habit of giving every dangerous mountain its own demon.

- **Meaning:** yaoguai, a demon or uncanny monster; usually an animal or spirit that has gained power through cultivation.
- **Source tradition:** Journey to the West; the Classic of Mountains and Seas; mountain folk tales.
- **What is not claimed:** no single named "Mountain Yaoguai" with its own myth; this is a type, not an individual.

### Xiezhi

The xiezhi — also rendered hsieh-chih or, in later tradition, often merged with the similar creature called the pixiu or the unicorn-like qilin in popular art — is the Chinese beast of justice. It is described as a creature like a goat or a unicorn with a single horn, sometimes with the body of a lion, and its office is to know guilt: in the oldest stories, the sage-king Shun's minister Gao Yao kept a xiezhi that would butt the guilty and spare the innocent, goring wrongdoers in trials where human judges could not tell truth from lies. The creature does not need evidence; it knows. Later Chinese law adopted it as an emblem — officials of the censorate wore the xiezhi on their robes, and statues of it stood at courts and gates, as if to say that judgment here was not merely human.

The xiezhi's horn is its instrument and its symbol: a single straight horn, lowered at the guilty, that does not miss and does not waver. In some accounts it could also speak or understand human language, and it refused food from the corrupt. Its calm is the point: unlike a raging demon, the xiezhi walks straight at its target without hurry, because it has already decided. In Korean tradition the same creature is the haetae, guardian of Seoul's palaces, and in Japanese art it appears as the kaichi — the justice beast traveling across East Asia with its office intact.

- **Also called:** hsieh-chih; Korean haetae; Japanese kaichi.
- **Defining traits:** single horn, goat- or lion-like body, unerring knowledge of guilt, a gore delivered after a fixed stare.
- **Office:** judge and law emblem; worn by censors, set at courts and gates.

### Hulijing

The hulijing is the Chinese fox spirit: a fox that, after long life and cultivation — a hundred years, a thousand years, by different counts — gains supernatural power, shapeshifting, and eventually, at nine tails, something close to divinity. The nine-tailed fox appears in the Classic of Mountains and Seas as an auspicious creature of Mount Qingqiu, whose cry is like a baby's and whose flesh, eaten, protects against gu poison; but in later tales the fox spirit is the great seducer and trickster of Chinese folklore, taking the form of a beautiful woman to lure, reward, or ruin men. The most famous is Daji, the fox spirit who, in the novel Investiture of the Gods, possesses the concubine of the last Shang king and drives his court to cruelty and ruin, becoming the archetype of the femme fatale in Chinese story.

Yet the fox spirit is never only a villain. In Pu Songling's Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio, fox spirits fall in love, repay kindness, keep faith, and suffer for it, and families kept fox shrines and offered them wine and food, hoping for favor and fearing offense in equal measure. A hulijing's power is measured in tails — more tails, more years, more power — and its character is measured by its cultivation: a fox that has used its power well may become a celestial servant, while one that has preyed on humans remains a demon. The lure is the creature's nature: it appears as what you most want to follow, and the question is always whether following is a gift or a trap.

- **Also called:** huli jing, fox spirit; nine-tailed form, jiuwei hu.
- **Defining traits:** fox form, human (usually beautiful) seeming, multiple tails marking age and power, fox-fire and illusion.
- **Major stories:** the Classic of Mountains and Seas; Daji in Investiture of the Gods; Pu Songling's fox tales.

### Mogwai

The mogwai of Chinese tradition — more properly mogui, "demon ghost" — is a small, rain-loving demon of folk belief, and its lore is genuinely thin and tangled. In Chinese sources the mogui is a minor demon, associated with rain and damp, that harms people and is warded off by various rites; it has no epic, no famous tale, and no consistent shape, and much of what English-speaking audiences know of the word comes from the 1984 film Gremlins, whose "mogwai" are furry, big-eared creatures with rules about water, sunlight, and feeding after midnight — rules invented for the film, not drawn from Chinese folklore. The film's creatures took the name and little else, and the two traditions should not be confused.

What can honestly be said is this: the Chinese mogui belongs to the crowded lower ranks of demonology, a small mischief-making spirit of wet places, of the kind that folk religion produced by the hundred — too minor for the great novels, too local for the classics, surviving in dictionaries, demon lists, and local custom rather than in story. Its defining folk associations are dampness, small size, and mischief toward the unwary traveler, especially in rain. A bestiary entry that gave it a full biography would be inventing one, so this entry marks the limit: a rain demon of the old lists, whose modern fame is borrowed from a film that is not its source.

- **Proper form:** mogui (Chinese), often rendered mogwai in English.
- **Folk associations:** rain, damp ground, small mischievous demons, harm to the unwary.
- **What is not claimed:** the Gremlins rules (no water, no sunlight, no feeding after midnight) are film invention, not Chinese folklore.

### River Long

*thin lore — director call*

The long — usually written "lung" in older romanization, "loong" in modern usage — is the Chinese dragon, and as a creature it has one of the richest lores in the world. But "River Long" as a distinct, named individual has no separate myth: the entry's thinness is a matter of naming, not of the dragon itself. The Chinese dragon is a composite of many animals — camel's head, deer's horns, rabbit's eyes, snake's neck, carp's scales, eagle's claws, tiger's paws — a serpentine, wingless creature that flies by its own power, commands rain and rivers, and embodies the emperor's authority and the fortune of the land. Unlike the Western dragon, it is generally benevolent: a bringer of rain, a guardian of water, a sign of good fortune, and the symbol of the emperor himself, whose robes bore five-clawed dragons that no one else might wear.

River dragons in particular are the lords of particular waters: each great river was said to have its dragon king (Longwang), who lived in a crystal palace beneath the waves, controlled rainfall, and received temples, offerings, and operas in times of drought. The carp that leaps the Dragon Gate waterfall becomes a dragon — the origin of the phrase for passing the imperial examinations — and dragons coil around temple pillars, dance at New Year, and race in boats at the Dragon Boat Festival. A river long, then, is a dragon whose ground is one river: powerful, serpentine, and tied to water's blessing and flood alike. The lore is vast; only the idea that one particular river long has its own separate biography is thin, and the entry says so rather than inventing a name for it.

- **Form:** long/lung, the Chinese dragon; serpentine, wingless, flying by its own power.
- **Defining traits:** composite anatomy, command of rain and rivers, benevolent and imperial associations, dragon kings (Longwang) of each water.
- **Related stories:** the carp and the Dragon Gate; the dragon kings and rain rituals; the dragon dance and boat festival.

### Taotie

The taotie is one of the most recognizable faces in Chinese art — and it is almost only a face. Cast in bronze on Shang and Zhou ritual vessels (ding, jue, and their kin) from the second millennium BCE, the taotie is a symmetrical, staring mask: great eyes, horns or brows, a gaping mouth, and often no body at all, or a body so compressed into the design that the face is all that can be read. Its name comes from later texts, which describe a creature of insatiable greed: in the Classic of Mountains and Seas and in the Zuo Zhuan's list of the four perils, the taotie is a being with a man's head and a sheep's or tiger's body that devours everything, even its own body, until only the head remains — which is why the bronze mask shows a head and nothing else.

Whether the bronze mask was ever meant to be that creature is debated by scholars; some argue it is a generalized animal mask, a protective or ritual design rather than a portrait of a myth. But the later legend stuck, and the taotie became the emblem of gluttony itself: a mouth that never closes, an appetite that eats the eater. It is one of the "four perils" (sixiong) of Chinese lore, alongside the chaos-beast Hundun, the confusion-bringer Qiongqi, and the liar Taowu — a quartet of beings that embody what a well-ordered world must exclude. In art the taotie stares out from vessels used in ancestral sacrifice, as if greed itself were set to guard the rites it threatened to consume.

- **Meaning:** taotie, the glutton; one of the four perils.
- **Defining traits:** great staring face, gaping mouth, little or no body — the appetite, not the animal.
- **Where it appears:** Shang and Zhou bronze ritual vessels; the Classic of Mountains and Seas; the Zuo Zhuan.

### Hundun

Hundun is chaos itself, given a body — or rather, given the absence of one. In the Classic of Mountains and Seas it is a creature like a dog or a bear with no face: no eyes, no ears, no mouth, no nose, but with six legs and four wings, living in the western mountains, delighting in confusion and in doing the opposite of what is right. It loves the wicked and hates the virtuous, it sings and dances in a way that makes no sense, and it is counted among the four perils alongside the taotie. In the Zhuangzi, Hundun is the emperor of the center, and the gods Shu (swift) and Hu (sudden) — the rulers of the southern and northern seas — repay his hospitality by boring seven holes in him, one for each sense, so that he can see, hear, eat, and breathe like other beings; on the seventh day Hundun dies. The parable is a warning: to impose order on chaos by force is to kill it, and chaos, left alone, was not evil so much as undifferentiated.

Hundun's name also became the Chinese word for the primeval chaos before heaven and earth divided — the undifferentiated blur from which the world emerged — so the creature and the cosmos share a name and a nature. It has no face to read, no direction to predict, and no motive that makes sense to the orderly: it is drawn, in the old texts, to the virtuous precisely because it resents order, and it leaves confusion in its wake the way a storm leaves broken branches. To meet Hundun in a bestiary is to meet a creature that cannot be faced, because there is no face to face — only a shambling, winged blur that turns the world sideways.

- **Meaning:** hundun, primeval chaos; one of the four perils.
- **Defining traits:** faceless, six legs, four wings, no sense organs in the Shanhaijing account; delights in confusion, favors the wicked, resents the virtuous.
- **Source stories:** the Classic of Mountains and Seas; the Zhuangzi's parable of the seven holes.

### Qilin, Corrupted

The qilin — also written ch'i-lin or kirin in Japanese — is the Chinese unicorn of good omen: a gentle, deer-like creature with a single horn (or antlered, dragon-like head in some art), a horse's or ox's body, a lion's or deer's coat patterned in five colors, and cloven hooves that tread so lightly it will not crush a blade of grass or harm a living insect. It appears only in times of peace and wise rule, at the birth or death of a sage — legend says it appeared to Confucius's mother before his birth, and a wounded qilin, caught by a hunter, was taken as a sign of the sage's coming death. It does not eat living flesh, it does not fight, and it is the emblem of benevolence, justice, and the arrival of a virtuous ruler. In Japan the kirin became a beer emblem and a symbol of good fortune; in Korea the girin guards palaces and temples.

A corrupted qilin is therefore a contradiction made flesh, and that contradiction is the point of this entry. In folklore the qilin does not turn evil — its nature is to appear and to bless, and to withdraw when the age is unworthy. Corruption is not a folk variant but the game's own turn: the auspicious beast, bent from its office, still wearing the horn and the five-colored coat but no longer gentle, no longer sparing the grass it walks on. The horror is not that a monster has appeared but that a good omen has been spoiled — the judge that now resents the righteous, the blessing that has learned to charge.

- **Abysmal take:** folklore gives no corrupted qilin; the corruption is Abysmal's own canon. The gentle, grass-sparing, sage-heralding qilin is the folklore; the charger that resents the righteous is the game's turn on it.
- **Also called:** ch'i-lin; Japanese kirin; Korean girin.
- **Defining traits (folklore):** single horn, five-colored coat, hooves that harm nothing, appears only for wise rulers and sages, eats no living flesh.

## Japanese Yōkai

Yōkai are Japan's folk supernatural: not gods, not quite demons, but the strange things that live alongside people — in old tools, in rivers, in mountains, in snow. The Edo-period artist Toriyama Sekien catalogued them in illustrated bestiaries that still shape how they look, and storytellers from Lafcadio Hearn to modern manga have kept them in circulation. A yōkai is often a warning, a joke, and a moral at once, and the same creature can be terrifying in one tale and comic in the next.

### Karakasa

The karakasa — full name karakasa-kozō, the "paper-umbrella boy" — is a tsukumogami, an old tool that has lived a hundred years and woken up. Japanese folk belief held that tools and household objects, after long service, could gain a spirit of their own, and the paper umbrella is the most cheerful of them: a single-legged, one-eyed umbrella with a long lolling tongue, hopping about on its handle-foot, grinning at travelers in the rain. It appears in Sekien's catalogues and in countless Edo prints and children's stories, usually as a prankster rather than a killer — it startles the unwary, hops in front of them on a dark road, and bounces away laughing.

The karakasa's comedy is the point. Where other tsukumogami — the sandal, the lute, the lantern — can be vengeful toward owners who threw them away, the umbrella boy is mischief without malice, a rainy-night surprise that tests whether the traveler keeps their nerve. Its single leg is its signature: it cannot walk, so it hops, and its hop is both its movement and its joke. In modern popular culture it has become one of the most recognizable yōkai of all, the mascot of the whole tsukumogami idea — that what we use every day, if we keep it long enough, might one day look back at us and grin.

- **Also called:** karakasa-kozō, karakasa-obake.
- **Kind:** tsukumogami — a tool spirit, born from a hundred-year-old object.
- **Defining traits:** one eye, one leg (the umbrella handle), long tongue, hopping movement, playful rather than murderous.

### Chōchin-obake

The chōchin-obake is the karakasa's lantern cousin and another tsukumogami: a paper lantern (chōchin) that has lived long enough to wake, splitting open into a wide mouth, sprouting one great eye, and growing a long tongue, and then floating about at night to startle travelers. Like the umbrella boy it belongs to Sekien's world of animated household things, and like him it is more prankster than predator — its trick is to appear where a light should be, to flare suddenly bright, and to grin with a mouth that should not be there. Travelers who meet it are meant to be frightened, not eaten, and the stories about it are usually short, comic, and told to children to explain why you should not be out alone after dark.

The lantern's meaning is in its light. A chōchin was the ordinary portable light of Edo Japan, carried on dark streets, and a lantern that moves by itself, that flares without a hand, is the familiar made strange — the night's own light turned against the walker. Its bamboo frame and oiled paper are still visible in its yōkai form, which is why it is recognizable at a glance: it is not a new monster but an old tool, misbehaving. In modern manga, games, and festival imagery the chōchin-obake is a stock yōkai, often paired with the karakasa as the two great tool spirits of the night road.

- **Also called:** chōchin-obake, lantern ghost.
- **Kind:** tsukumogami — a tool spirit, born from a long-used paper lantern.
- **Defining traits:** paper lantern body, bamboo frame, one eye, split mouth, long tongue, floating drift, sudden flare.

### Kappa

The kappa is Japan's most famous water imp: a small, turtle-like creature with a shell on its back, webbed hands and feet, a beak-like mouth, and a shallow dish or depression on top of its head that holds water — the source of its strength. So long as the dish is full, the kappa is strong; spill the water, and it weakens or is forced to bow, and bowing spills the dish further, which is why the traditional defense against a kappa is to bow politely to it. Kappa live in rivers and ponds, and in folk tales they are dangerous and comic at once: they drown horses and children, pull swimmers under, and demand cucumbers — their favorite food, so much so that cucumber sushi rolls are still called kappamaki — and they are obsessed with a mythical ball inside the human body, the shirikodama, which they are said to steal from the unwary.

Yet kappa are also bound by a strict, almost legalistic politeness. They keep promises, they can be bargained with, and a kappa that has been helped or outwitted may teach a human bone-setting or medicine in return — several villages credited kappa with teaching their healers. They are, in short, the river's own trickster: deadly to the careless, respectful to the courteous, and obsessed with water, cucumbers, and wrestling. The dish on the head is the whole creature in miniature — a being that carries its river with it wherever it goes, and that dries out when it leaves the water behind.

- **Defining traits:** turtle shell, webbed limbs, beaked mouth, head dish holding water, love of cucumbers, skill at wrestling.
- **Weakness in folklore:** the head dish — spill it, by a bow or by trickery, and the kappa weakens.
- **Temperament:** drowns the careless, keeps promises to the polite, sometimes teaches medicine in return for kindness.

### Oni

The oni is Japan's ogre-demon: a huge, horned, often red- or blue-skinned figure with a tiger-skin loincloth and an iron club (kanabō), living in mountains, on remote islands, or in hell itself, where it torments the wicked. Oni appear in the oldest Japanese stories as the enemies heroes must defeat — Momotarō, the Peach Boy, sails to Oni Island to reclaim treasure from them — and in Setsubun, the spring festival, households throw roasted soybeans while shouting "Oni out, fortune in!" to drive them from the house. They are brute force made into a person: strong, loud, fond of sake and cruelty, and often surprisingly easy to outwit, as in the tales where a hero tricks an oni out of its magic cloak, its treasure, or its own strength.

But oni are not only villains. They guard temples as fierce protector figures, they appear in festivals as bringers of luck when properly driven out, and in some tales an oni repents, converts, or serves a hero. The most famous oni of all is Shuten-dōji, the great demon of Mount Ōe who drank sake, abducted maidens, and was finally slain by Minamoto no Yorimitsu and his companions after they drugged him with poisoned sake — a story told in picture scrolls and Noh plays for centuries. The oni's horns, club, and tiger skin are its uniform, and its character is its appetite: for drink, for flesh, for a fight, and for being the biggest thing in the room.

- **Apex note:** the apex Shuten-dōji is a separate entry, covered in the apex lore; this entry is the oni kind, not the demon king of Mount Ōe.
- **Defining traits:** horns, red or blue skin, tiger-skin loincloth, iron club (kanabō), great strength, love of sake.
- **Cultural role:** villain of Momotarō and Shuten-dōji tales; driven out at Setsubun ("Oni out, fortune in!"); temple guardian in fierce form.

### Bake-danuki

The bake-danuki is the shapeshifting tanuki — the Japanese raccoon dog — grown magical with age and mischief. Tanuki are real animals of the Japanese countryside, and in folklore they are the great tricksters: they inflate their scrotums to enormous size in art and story (a comic emblem of their power, not to be taken solemnly), they drum on their bellies to lure travelers, they transform into humans, teakettles, trees, and one-eyed monks, and they lead the unwary astray with phantom processions and false inns. The most famous tanuki tale is Bunbuku Chagama, in which a tanuki transforms into a teakettle to repay a man's kindness, and the temple that keeps it finds the kettle sprouting legs and a tail when heated.

Unlike the kitsune, whose trickery often turns cruel or amorous, the tanuki's is usually comic and good-natured — it wants a laugh, a drink, and a meal, and it is as likely to be tricked itself as to trick others. But bake-danuki can be dangerous too: in some tales they lure travelers to cliffs, possess people, or serve as the familiar of a darker power. Their shapeshifting is their nature: a tanuki is never quite what it seems, and the drumbeat of its belly is the sound of a transformation beginning. In modern Japan the tanuki statue outside a restaurant or bar, sake bottle in hand and belly out, is a sign of good fortune and hospitality — the trickster domesticated into a welcome.

- **Also called:** bake-danuki, tanuki (the animal and the yōkai share the name).
- **Defining traits:** raccoon-dog form, shapeshifting into humans and objects, belly-drumming, love of sake and pranks, usually comic rather than cruel.
- **Famous tale:** Bunbuku Chagama, the teakettle tanuki.

### Kasha

The kasha — also called kasha, "fire cart," in some tellings — is a yōkai that steals corpses. It appears as a great fiery cat, or as a demon riding a burning cart, that descends on funerals and snatches the body of the newly dead, especially the bodies of the wicked, whose corpses it is said to drag to hell. In some accounts the kasha is a cat that has lived long and turned demonic; in others it is a servant of hell sent to claim a soul that has not been properly mourned or whose sins demand a harsher fate. The sign of its coming is corpse-fire: pale, floating flames (hitodama) around a funeral, and the sound of a cart's wheels in the night air.

The kasha's horror is specific: it does not kill the living but desecrates the dead, and it strikes at the moment when the living are most vulnerable — at a funeral, in grief, with the body unguarded. Folk countermeasures were therefore funeral measures: two offerings of food at a wake to distract it, guards posted at the coffin, and prayers to keep the body safe until burial. In art the kasha is shown as a cat wreathed in flame, riding or pulling a cart of fire, its claws out for the corpse it has come to claim. It is the funeral's own nightmare: the fear that even in death, a person is not safe from being taken.

- **Also called:** kasha, "fire cart" demon in some traditions.
- **Defining traits:** fiery cat form or demon on a burning cart, corpse-fire flames, appears at funerals, steals corpses — especially of the wicked.
- **Folk countermeasures:** doubled funeral offerings, guards at the coffin, prayers for the dead.

### Kitsune

The kitsune is Japan's fox spirit, and one of the most complex yōkai in the tradition: a fox that gains a tail for every hundred years of life, up to nine, growing wiser and more powerful with each, until the nine-tailed fox becomes nearly divine. Kitsune are messengers and servants of Inari, god of rice, prosperity, and foxes, and Inari's shrines are guarded by stone foxes holding keys, scrolls, or jewels in their mouths. But wild kitsune are tricksters: they shapeshift into beautiful women, bewitch travelers, create phantom fires (kitsune-bi) that lead the lost astray, and possess people — kitsune-tsuki, fox possession, was once a recognized folk explanation for illness and strange behavior, and families accused of harboring foxes were feared as kitsune-mochi.

The two faces of the kitsune are held together in story. In some tales a kitsune wife lives happily with a human husband until he discovers her tail and she must flee, leaving him with a child and a promise; in others a kitsune seduces, ruins, or eats a traveler, and in the great Tamamo-no-Mae legend a nine-tailed fox in the form of a court lady corrupts an emperor before being hunted down and killed, her spirit becoming the killing stone (sesshōseki) that poisons all who touch it. The fox's power is illusion — fox-fire, false seemings, afterimages — and its character is measured, like the Chinese hulijing, by whether it uses that power kindly or cruelly. A kitsune is never just a fox: it is a fox that has lived long enough to have a biography.

- **Defining traits:** fox form, up to nine tails marking age and power, shapeshifting (especially into a beautiful woman), fox-fire (kitsune-bi), illusion.
- **Sacred link:** Inari, god of rice and foxes; stone fox guardians at Inari shrines.
- **Major stories:** the kitsune wife; Tamamo-no-Mae and the killing stone; fox possession (kitsune-tsuki) in folk belief.

### Tengu

The tengu are the mountain goblins of Japan: originally fierce, bird-like demons with beaks and wings, later shown as long-nosed, red-faced men in mountain ascetic dress, carrying a feather fan (ha-uchiwa) that can summon winds and a sword, and living in the high mountains where they train warriors and torment the proud. In early tales tengu are dangerous: they abduct children and monks (kamikakushi, "spirited away"), possess the arrogant, and cause madness and disaster. In later tradition they soften into the yamabushi, mountain ascetics, who are stern teachers rather than killers — the great swordsman Minamoto no Yoshitsune is said to have learned swordsmanship from the tengu king Sōjōbō on Mount Kurama, and tengu became patrons of martial arts, proud, disciplined, and contemptuous of vanity.

The tengu's defining vice is pride: they punish the arrogant, the boastful, and the noisy, and they reward humility and discipline. Their fan is their emblem — a wave of it can raise a gale, flatten a forest, or blow a traveler off a path — and their mountain is their ground, which they do not leave for the lowlands. In art there are two great kinds: the karasu-tengu, crow-like, beaked and feathered, and the hanadaka-tengu, long-nosed and human-faced, and both wear the garb of the mountain priest. To meet a tengu is to be judged: are you proud enough to be punished, or humble enough to be taught?

- **Kinds:** karasu-tengu (crow-like, beaked) and hanadaka-tengu (long-nosed, human-faced).
- **Defining traits:** wings, feather fan that summons wind, sword, mountain ascetic dress, contempt for pride.
- **Major stories:** Sōjōbō training Yoshitsune on Mount Kurama; tengu abductions (kamikakushi).

### Yuki-onna

Yuki-onna, the snow woman, is one of Japan's most haunting yōkai: a beautiful woman in a white kimono, with pale skin, dark hair, and eyes that seem to look through you, who appears in snowstorms to travelers lost in the mountains, and whose breath freezes those she touches. Lafcadio Hearn's retelling is the most famous: a woodcutter and his old master shelter in a hut during a blizzard, and Yuki-onna comes in the night, freezes the old man with her breath, and spares the young man on condition that he never tell anyone what he saw — years later, married with children, he tells his wife the story, and she reveals herself as Yuki-onna, spares him for the children's sake, and vanishes into the snow, never to return.

Yuki-onna is both murderer and mourner: in some tales she kills without mercy, luring travelers to freeze; in others she falls in love, marries, and lives as a human wife until the secret is broken, and her departure is a tragedy rather than a punishment. Her beauty is the snow's beauty — perfect, cold, and fatal to stay in — and her silence is the silence of a blizzard, in which no footprints are left and no voice carries. She belongs to the mountains in winter, to the moment when a traveler realizes he is lost and the snow is no longer falling on him but through him.

- **Also called:** yuki-onna, snow woman; also yuki-musume (snow girl) in some regions.
- **Defining traits:** white kimono, pale skin, freezing breath and touch, appears in blizzards, leaves no footprints.
- **Famous tale:** Hearn's "Yuki-onna," the woodcutter, the vow, and the wife revealed.

### Jorōgumo

Jorōgumo, the "entangling bride" or "whore spider," is a spider yōkai that takes the form of a beautiful woman to lure men to her web. In its true form it is a great golden orb-weaver spider (the jorō spider of Japan, Nephila clavata, is named for it); in its seeming it is a lovely woman, often playing a biwa lute, who invites a traveler to her house, to a waterfall, or to a quiet room, and then binds him in silk and devours him. The most famous stories place her at waterfalls — notably Jōren Falls in Izu — where she is said to live in the plunge pool and to lure woodcutters and travelers with her music and her beauty, and where local tradition once warned against approaching the water alone.

Jorōgumo belongs to a family of spider women in Japanese lore, and her horror is the oldest one: the beautiful stranger who is not what she seems, the invitation that is a trap, the silk that looks like a gift and tightens like a snare. She is not a mindless beast but a patient hunter: she waits, she plays, she speaks sweetly, and only when the victim is close and alone does the web spring. In some tales she can be bargained with or outwitted — a woodcutter who refuses her lure, or who cuts his way free, survives — but the stories agree on the warning: do not follow a beautiful stranger to a lonely waterfall, and do not trust silk you cannot see.

- **Also called:** jorōgumo, "entangling bride."
- **True form:** great golden orb-weaver spider; seeming form, a beautiful woman, often with a biwa.
- **Haunts:** waterfalls (notably Jōren Falls), lonely houses, and quiet waters where a traveler can be lured alone.

### Nure-onna

Nure-onna, the "wet woman," is a serpent-bodied yōkai of rivers and shores: a woman's upper body and a snake's long, wet tail, with damp, stringy hair and eyes like a snake's, who appears near water at night. Her most famous trick is the burden: she appears to a traveler carrying what looks like a baby, begs him to hold it for a moment, and if he takes it, the child grows impossibly heavy, rooting him to the spot while she coils around him and drains his blood or drags him into the water. In other tales she appears with the ushi-oni, the ox-demon, whose coming she announces — the wet woman on the shore is the sign that something worse is in the water behind her.

Nure-onna's horror is the abuse of pity: she asks for help, and help is the trap. The traditional defense is refusal — do not take the bundle, do not approach the wet woman on the shore, and keep your distance from the water at night — and the tales that end well are the ones in which the traveler refuses and walks on. Her wetness is her nature: she is never dry, never far from the river or the sea, and her hair is always dripping, as if she has just climbed out of the water and will return to it as soon as her work is done.

- **Also called:** nure-onna, "wet woman."
- **Defining traits:** woman's upper body, serpent's tail, wet stringy hair, snake eyes, appears near water at night.
- **Signature trick:** the false baby — a burden that grows heavy and roots the helper in place while she coils.

### Nue

The nue is a chimera of the Japanese night: the face of a monkey, the body of a tanuki (or tiger, in some accounts), the legs of a tiger, and the tail of a serpent, whose eerie cry — rendered "hyoo-hyoo" in the old tales — brings illness and ill fortune to all who hear it. Its great story is in the Tale of the Heike: in the summer of 1153, a black cloud settled over the imperial palace at night, the emperor fell ill, and the warrior Minamoto no Yorimasa shot the nue from the sky with an arrow, after which the emperor recovered and Yorimasa was rewarded with a sword. The creature's body was then put in a boat and set adrift on the river, and the places where it washed ashore were said to be cursed.

The nue's cry is its weapon and its warning: in the Heike it is heard before the cloud appears, and in later tradition the word nue itself came to mean something uncanny and ill-defined — a "nue-like" thing is a thing that cannot be named or pinned down. Its mixed body is the point: monkey, tiger, tanuki, serpent, all at once, a creature that cannot be read because it has no single nature to read. It belongs to the night cloud, to the emperor's sickroom, and to the moment when a court realizes that something is wrong but cannot say what — and it fades when it is shot down, as a bad dream fades at waking.

- **Defining traits:** monkey face, tanuki or tiger body, tiger legs, serpent tail, eerie night cry that brings illness.
- **Source story:** the Tale of the Heike, 1153 — Yorimasa shoots the nue from the palace sky.
- **Cultural echo:** "nue-like" (nue-teki) for something uncanny and hard to define.

### Tsuchigumo

The tsuchigumo, the "earth spider," is the great ground spider of Japanese legend: a monstrous spider that lives in the earth, in caves, or in a webbed lair, and that preys on heroes and travelers who enter its ground. In the oldest tales the name was also used for the indigenous peoples who resisted the Yamato court — "earth spiders" as a slur for those who lived in pit dwellings and would not submit — and the legends blend the two: the hero Minamoto no Yorimitsu, already famous for slaying Shuten-dōji, is said to have fallen ill, and when a monk came to tend him, Yorimitsu saw through the disguise to a great spider, cut it down, and followed its blood trail to a cave where the true tsuchigumo lay in its web, which he and his men destroyed. In related tellings, Yorimitsu — also known by the name Raikō — faces a tsuchigumo whose webs snare even heroes.

As a yōkai the tsuchigumo is the opposite of the jorōgumo's lure: it does not seduce, it holds ground. Its webs are vast, its legs are crushing, and its lair is its kingdom — a place where the earth itself seems to be woven shut, and where a hero must cut his way in and out. It is the spider as a territorial power, not a trickster: slow, inexorable, and patient, waiting in the dark of its cave for the footstep that tells it prey has entered the web. Where the jorōgumo is the beautiful stranger, the tsuchigumo is the ground itself, grown legs and hunger.

- **Also called:** tsuchigumo, "earth spider."
- **Defining traits:** colossal ground spider, vast webs, earth or cave lair, crushing legs, territorial rather than luring.
- **Source stories:** the Yorimitsu (Raikō) legends; the earth-spider tales of the Yamato court's frontier wars.

# Bestiary lore — The Undead, Celestials & the Fallen, Demons, Devils & the Damned

Docs-only lore companion to `behavior-specs/part-4.md`. This document is the lore home: it gathers, in the writer's own words, the folklore, myth, and literary tradition behind each creature. It sets no stats and no game rules. Where Abysmal's game canon deliberately departs from folklore, the entry says so in a short "Abysmal take" line. Where the lore behind a name is genuinely thin, contradictory, or a modern composite rather than a named figure from tradition, the entry says so honestly and is marked "thin lore — director call" instead of inventing a fake mythology. Each creature appears exactly once, in its listed family; overlap tags from the tracker are noted inside the relevant entry, not given a second entry.

## The Undead

The dead who will not stay dead are among the oldest monsters in the world, and they are not one kind of thing. Some are bodies that walk, some are spirits without bodies, some are curses that wear a dead man's shape, and some are the dead as a whole people — a host, a choir, a battlefield's worth of bones standing up at once. What most of them share is unfinished business: a grave robbed, a burial denied, a wrong unavenged, a soul that never got where it was going.

### Skeleton

The animated skeleton is less a single folklore creature than a shape that keeps recurring wherever people have thought hard about death. Medieval Europe painted it everywhere in the Danse Macabre, the Dance of Death, in which skeletons lead pope and peasant alike in a final procession — a reminder that death levels every rank. Churchyards, charnel houses, and ossuaries made the image familiar: stacked bones, skulls grinning from walls, the dead literally on display. From there the skeleton entered carnival, theatre, and story as the most legible possible ghost — a person with everything soft taken away, leaving only the frame that once held them up.

In folk belief, a skeleton that moves on its own is usually a soul that has not been laid to rest, or a body raised by someone else's will — a witch, a sorcerer, a necromancer in the old sense of one who speaks with the dead. It is rarely cunning and rarely personal. It does not hate you in particular; it simply has not stopped. That blank persistence is the point, and it is why skeletons so often appear in groups: one dead man is a tragedy, but a field of them is an army that never needs food, sleep, or pay.

- **Variants:** the grinning dancer of the Danse Macabre; the churchyard skeleton of ghost stories; the sorcerer's risen labourer of later fantasy.
- **Defining traits:** bare bone, empty eyes, a gait that is all joints and no flesh, and a total indifference to fear, pain, or pleading.

### Skeleton Archer

There is no single named "skeleton archer" in old folklore; the figure is a natural extension of the animated skeleton into the world of dead armies. Wherever stories imagine the dead rising to fight — the battlefield dead, the soldiers of a cursed host, the guardians of a tomb — some of them must have carried bows in life, and the dead, in these stories, tend to keep doing what they did in life. Archery also suits the skeleton's particular horror: it kills at a distance, without ever coming close enough to reason with, and an arrow loosed by something with no muscles and no breath feels wrong in a way a sword blow does not.

The archer's closest cousins are the ghostly hosts of European legend — the Wild Hunt and its many local forms, in which dead hunters ride the night sky — and the tomb guardians of Egyptian and Near Eastern stories, where the dead are set to watch over what was buried with them. In all of them, the dead do not fight for a cause. They fight because they were placed there, and placement, for the dead, is permanent.

- **Variants:** the dead bowman of a battlefield host; the tomb guardian who shoots intruders; the spectral hunter of the night sky.
- **Defining traits:** distance kept deliberately, patience, and arrows that arrive from a figure that should not be able to draw a bow at all.

### Zombie

The zombie, in its oldest form, has nothing to do with plagues or apocalypses. It comes from Haitian Vodou tradition, rooted in West and Central African beliefs, where a zombie is a dead person reanimated by a sorcerer, the bokor, and stripped of will — a body put to work, not a monster on the loose. The horror of that zombie is not hunger but unfreedom: a person made into a tool, aware or half-aware, labouring without rest. Early accounts stressed the social fear behind the story — of being stolen from your grave and your family and set to work in a field you never chose.

The flesh-eating, shambling horde is a later invention, and it owes more to the ghoul (see below) than to Haiti. George Romero's *Night of the Living Dead* (1968) famously called its creatures ghouls; audiences and journalists renamed them zombies, and the name stuck to the new shape. From Romero onward, the zombie became a crowd: slow alone, unstoppable together, a disaster in human shape. The two traditions now share a word and almost nothing else, and a bestiary that uses both should keep them distinct.

- **Variants:** the Haitian zombie, a will-less labourer raised by sorcery; the Romero horde, a mindless, multiplying crowd; the voodoo-adjacent "zombi" of Caribbean and Louisiana lore.
- **Defining traits:** in the old tradition, obedience without will; in the modern one, hunger without thought, and numbers that matter more than any single body.

### Skeleton Knight

Like the Skeleton Archer, the Skeleton Knight is a composite figure rather than a named creature of folklore — but the pieces it is made from are very old. The armoured dead man belongs to the same world as the barrow wight and the draugr: the warrior who was buried in his armour, with his sword, and who kept both. Chivalric romance and later gothic fiction loved the image of a knight who could not lay down his arms even in death, and ghost stories of haunted castles and battlefields are full of phantom knights still patrolling ground they died defending.

What makes the knight distinct from a common skeleton is rank and memory. A skeleton is anyone; a knight is someone who had a code, a lord, a banner, and a war. Stories about such dead men usually give them a reason to linger: an oath unkept, a defeat unavenged, a charge never called off. The armour matters too. In life it marked a man who could afford to be hard to kill; in death it marks a body that refuses, even as bone, to be soft.

- **Variants:** the phantom knight of a haunted battlefield; the tomb guardian in full harness; the cursed champion bound to a place by an oath.
- **Defining traits:** armour worn past death, a sword kept in hand, a deliberate and formal bearing, and a loyalty — to a place, a lord, or a cause — that outlived its body.

### Ghoul

*Overlap note: also tagged Demons, Devils & the Damned in the tracker; written here, once.*

The ghoul begins in pre-Islamic Arabian tradition as the *ghūl*, from a root meaning "to seize." It is a desert demon, counted among the jinn, and its first and most important trick is deception: it takes a pleasing or familiar shape — a woman by the roadside, a companion, a light in the waste — and lures a lone traveller away from the road and from other people, then kills and eats them. Early accounts stress that the ghoul hunts the living, in lonely places, and that it is a living supernatural creature, not itself a corpse. The female form, the *ghulah*, is especially prominent, and some accounts give ghouls the shape of a hyena, an animal already associated with graveyards and carrion.

The corpse-eating graveyard ghoul that English speakers picture is largely a later, Western reshaping. Antoine Galland's early-eighteenth-century French translation of the *One Thousand and One Nights* added the detail of ghouls digging in burial grounds for dead bodies, and that image proved so sticky that it swallowed the older desert deceiver almost whole. William Beckford's *Vathek* (1786) brought the word into English literature, and from there the ghoul drifted into the Western cemetery, where it eventually lent its hunger to Romero's zombies.

- **Variants:** the Arabian *ghūl*, a shapeshifting desert lure; the *ghulah*, its female form; the Western graveyard ghoul, a corpse-eater of Galland's invention; the modern horror ghoul, a bestial, flesh-hungry humanoid.
- **Defining traits:** shapeshifting and luring in the old tradition; carrion hunger in the new one; in both, an appetite for human flesh and a home at the edges — desert, ruin, graveyard — where people are alone.

*Abysmal take: Abysmal files the Ghoul with the Undead, following the Western graveyard shape; the older Arabian ghūl was a living demon of the jinn, not a risen corpse.*

### Barrow Wight

The barrow wight is a name with a double life. The word "wight" is Old English *wiht*, simply a creature or being, and "barrow" is a burial mound; the compound as a monster's name was coined in the nineteenth century and made famous by J. R. R. Tolkien, whose barrow-wights haunt the Barrow-downs in *The Fellowship of the Ring* — cold, pale, spell-working dead who lure travellers into the mounds, lay them out like corpses, and drain their warmth and will. Tolkien drew on a much older northern idea: the mound-dweller, the dead man who stays in his grave and guards what was buried with him.

That older idea is the Norse *haugbúi*, the howe-dweller, the dead body living on within its tomb, rarely found far from its burial place — and its roaming cousin, the draugr (see below). Anglo-Saxon and Norse burial practice, with grave goods, weapons, and treasure laid in mounds, made the guardian a practical fear as well as a supernatural one: robbing a mound meant facing whatever was left in it. The barrow wight is that fear given a shape — not a wandering monster but a place that fights back, a grave that refuses to be opened.

- **Variants:** Tolkien's Barrow-downs wight, cold and spell-bound; the Norse *haugbúi*, the mound-bound dead; the folk "mound guardian" of British barrow lore.
- **Defining traits:** bound to its mound, cold to the touch and to the spirit, armed with what was buried with it, and hostile above all to intruders and robbers.

### Specter

"Specter" is the generalist of ghost words — from the Latin *spectrum*, a thing seen, an apparition — and that generality is the point. A specter is not a particular kind of dead person with a particular story; it is the experience of seeing something that should not be there, given a name. In ghost lore across Europe and America, specters are described as pale, insubstantial, drifting figures, sometimes repeating a gesture or a route, sometimes simply present, watching, in a place where someone died badly or suddenly.

Because the word covers so much, specter stories tend to agree on only a few traits: the figure is visible but untouchable, or touchable only in the worst way; it is tied to a place rather than free to roam; and it is indifferent to the living in a way that feels colder than hostility. It does not want revenge, like a revenant, and it does not warn, like a banshee. It is simply still there, the way a stain is still there after the thing that made it is gone.

- **Variants:** the pale drifting figure of a haunted house; the roadside apparition that repeats a walk; the battlefield specter seen by whole companies at once.
- **Defining traits:** translucency, silence or near-silence, attachment to a haunt, and a presence that feels less like a person than like a leftover.

### Poltergeist

The poltergeist — German for "noisy ghost" — is the ghost that does not bother to appear. Where other spirits are seen, the poltergeist is heard and felt: knocking, rapping, footsteps with no walker, objects lifted, thrown, or smashed, stones falling inside closed rooms. The classic cases cluster around a household, often one with an adolescent at its centre, and around a period of weeks or months rather than a single night. The famous Enfield case in London in the late 1970s is only the best-publicised of a long line; reports of noisy, object-throwing spirits run back through early modern witchcraft pamphlets and into medieval chronicles.

Folklorists and investigators have long argued about what a poltergeist is: a spirit, a fraud, or — in the most interesting theory — a living person's own suppressed energy, thrown outward without their knowledge. That ambiguity is part of the lore. Unlike a ghost with a story, the poltergeist has no face and no motive you can negotiate with. It is disturbance itself, and the only consistent advice in the stories is practical: leave the room, remove the objects, wait it out, or find the person at the centre of it.

- **Variants:** the household knocker of German and English folklore; the stone-throwing spirit of early modern pamphlets; the modern "recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis" case, centred on a living adolescent.
- **Defining traits:** invisibility, noise, thrown objects, a focus on one house or one person, and activity that flares with disturbance and fades with calm.

### Draugr

*Overlap note: also tagged Norse & Germanic in the tracker; written here, once.*

The draugr is the walking dead of the Icelandic sagas, and the sagas are blunt about what makes one. A draugr is a corpse that walks again — Old Norse *aptrgangr*, "one who walks after death" — and the men who become draugar are usually men who were greedy, cruel, or overbearing in life, or who clung too hard to their treasure and reputation. Grettir's fight with Glámr in *Grettis saga* is the famous case: Glámr, a shepherd who died in winter and refused the fast, comes back with moon-bright eyes and impossible strength, breaks into farmhouses, kills livestock and men, and curses Grettir so that his strength will never grow again. In *Eyrbyggja saga*, Thorolf Halt-foot is dug up swollen to the size of an ox, undecayed, blue-black — *hel-blár*, "black as death" — and so heavy that levers are needed to move him.

Draugar are bodies, not spirits, and that is what makes them frightening in the sagas. They crush, they sit on sleepers until they suffocate, they guard their mounds and their hoards, and they bring disease, bad weather, and madness in their wake. Later Icelandic folktales added shape-shifting — a seal, a flayed bull, a grey horse with no ears or tail, a cat that grows heavier on a sleeper's chest — and the power to move through solid earth. The mound-dwelling *haugbúi*, who never leaves his grave, is the draugr's stay-at-home cousin; the draugr is the one who comes out.

- **Variants:** the roaming *aptrgangr* of the Icelandic sagas; the mound-bound *haugbúi*; the sea-draug of coastal stories; the later folktale shape-shifter.
- **Defining traits:** a swollen, blue-black or corpse-pale body; enormous weight and strength; a hoard or a grudge; and a stubborn, personal malice that a mere skeleton lacks.

### Mummy

*Overlap note: also tagged Egyptian & Near East in the tracker; written here, once.*

The mummy enters monster lore by two doors at once. The first is real: Egyptian mummification, the careful preservation of the body for the afterlife, with linen wrappings, amulets, and a tomb built to last forever. The second is modern and mostly invented: the "mummy's curse," which exploded into public imagination after the opening of Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922 and the deaths that followed, deaths that newspapers eagerly tied to a curse even though no such curse was inscribed in the tomb. Before that, "mummy" in European usage often meant something cruder — powdered mummy sold as medicine, and mummies unwrapped at parties as entertainment — a reminder of how casually the nineteenth century treated the dead of other cultures.

As a monster, the mummy is slow, bandaged, and unstoppable in the way of all the best undead: it does not need to hurry, because it has already waited thousands of years. Its horror is not speed but patience and violation — a body prepared with immense care for a sacred purpose, dragged back into a world that treats it as a curiosity. In fiction from the late nineteenth century onward, the mummy usually rises because its tomb has been robbed, and it pursues the robbers with a single-mindedness that makes it less a villain than a consequence.

- **Variants:** the Egyptian tomb guardian of fiction; the reanimated priest or pharaoh of Victorian and early film stories; the "curse" itself, a doom that follows the robber rather than a body that chases him.
- **Defining traits:** linen wrappings, slow and inexorable movement, a tomb that must not be entered, and a curse that attaches to theft and desecration.

*Abysmal take: the mummy's spoken curse at range is game canon; in folklore the curse is a doom that follows tomb-robbers, not a ranged attack cast in combat.*

### Phantom

*Thin lore — director call.* "Phantom" is, like "specter," a general word for an apparition — from the Greek *phantasma*, a thing that appears — and it has no single folklore creature attached to it. The behaviour specs already flag this entry as thin, and the research bears that out: phantoms appear everywhere in ghost literature and nowhere as one distinct being with its own stories, rules, or regional variants. The Phantom of the Opera is a living man in a mask; the phantom of a haunted house is usually, on inspection, a specter, a wraith, or a ghost with a more specific name.

What can honestly be said is that "phantom" in ghost tradition marks a particular quality of apparition: silent, gliding, and watchful, more often seen at a distance or at the edge of vision than confronted up close. It is the ghost as pure appearance — something seen that will not resolve into a person when you approach it. That quality is real and useful, but it is a quality, not a creature, and any bestiary entry for the Phantom should be read as a description of that quality rather than as a figure from a named tradition.

- **Variants:** none distinct; the word overlaps specter, wraith, shade, and apparition in most sources.
- **Defining traits:** silence, gliding movement, a tendency to be seen circling or at a distance, and a refusal to resolve into anything solid or speakable.

### Banshee

*Overlap note: also tagged Celtic & British Isles in the tracker; written here, once.*

The banshee — Irish *bean sí*, "woman of the fairy mound," Scottish Gaelic *bean sith* — is not a monster that attacks. She is a messenger, and her message is death. In Irish and Scottish tradition, the banshee's wail or keen is heard when someone is about to die, especially a member of one of the old families to which a banshee was attached. The keen itself is the point: it is the ritual lament for the dead, the *caoine*, performed in folklore by a supernatural woman rather than by human mourners. To hear it is to know that a death is coming; it is not, in the old stories, the thing that causes the death.

Descriptions vary, and that variation is itself traditional. Sometimes she is a young woman with long hair, sometimes an old hag, sometimes washing blood from clothes at a stream — the *bean-nighe*, the washerwoman, her Scottish cousin, who washes the grave-clothes of those about to die. Sometimes there are many banshees keening together. What stays constant is the sound: a long, rising, unearthly wail in the night, and the dread that follows it. The banshee belongs to the fairy world, the *sídhe*, not to the world of corpses; she is a fairy herald of death, not a dead woman herself.

- **Variants:** the Irish *bean sí* attached to old families; the Scottish *bean sith* and *bean-nighe*, the washer at the ford; the keening woman heard in groups before a battle or a great death.
- **Defining traits:** the wail itself; a female figure, young or old; an association with particular families and with fairy mounds; and a role as omen, not executioner.

*Abysmal take: Abysmal turns the banshee's wail into an attack with a wind-up the player can interrupt or flee; in folklore the wail announces a death, it does not inflict one.*

### Vampire

The vampire is two creatures wearing one name. The older one is the Slavic and Balkan revenant: a corpse that leaves its grave at night to feed on family, livestock, and neighbours, blamed for sickness and sudden deaths, and dealt with by the community — stake, beheading, burning, and a long list of preventives including garlic, crosses, and seeds scattered before the door for the creature to stop and count. These vampires were not elegant. They were bloated, ruddy, restless dead, closer to a plague than to a person, and the word covered a whole family of restless corpses across Eastern Europe.

The second vampire is the literary one, and it largely replaced the first. John Polidori's Lord Ruthven (1819) made the vampire an aristocrat; Bram Stoker's *Dracula* (1897) made him the vampire, a Transylvanian count with a castle, a cape, and a will of iron. From Stoker onward, the vampire became charming, intelligent, and tragic — a predator who walks into your house like a guest because, in the oldest rule of the stories, he must be invited. Modern fiction has kept both halves in tension: the vampire as monster and the vampire as person, and the best vampire stories are about the gap between them.

- **Variants:** the Slavic revenant, a bloated corpse feeding on kin; the *nosferatu* of Romanian and Greek tradition; the literary aristocrat from Polidori to Stoker; the modern sympathetic vampire of twentieth- and twenty-first-century fiction.
- **Defining traits:** blood-drinking; a return from the grave tied to family and place; vulnerability to sunlight, garlic, holy symbols, and the stake in various combinations depending on the tradition; and, in the literary line, charm, patience, and a terrible politeness.

### Strigoi

The strigoi belong to Romanian tradition, and they come in two forms that must not be confused. The *strigoi vii* — the living strigoi — is a witch, a living person who can send their soul out at night to meet with other witches and with the dead, and who will become a vampire after death. The *strigoi mort* — the dead strigoi — is the reanimated body itself, returning to suck the blood of family, livestock, and neighbours, and blamed for sickness and death in the community. The word comes from Romanian *striga*, witch, from Latin *strix*, a screech owl and, in Roman belief, a night demon that attacked children — the same root that gives Italian *strega*, witch.

A person was thought marked for becoming a strigoi by signs at birth or death: born with a caul, born with a tail, born out of wedlock, dead an unnatural death, or dead before baptism. Strigoi could change shape, pass through barriers, and become invisible, and they were dealt with much like other Slavic vampires — exhumation, staking, and burning. They are close kin to the *moroi*, a related Romanian term used especially in Wallachia, and to the werewolf-like *pricolici* and *vârcolac* of neighbouring traditions. The behaviour specs flag this entry as thin lore, but the Romanian material is in fact substantial; the confusion comes from the word covering both a living witch and a dead vampire, and from modern fiction flattening both into a generic vampire.

- **Variants:** *strigoi vii*, the living witch; *strigoi mort* (plural *strigoi morti*), the dead vampire; *strigoaica*, the female form; the related *moroi*.
- **Defining traits:** a double nature, living witch and dead revenant; shape-shifting and invisibility; a mark at birth or death; and a hunger that falls first on kin.

### Jiangshi

*Overlap note: also tagged Chinese & East Asian in the tracker; written here, once.*

The jiangshi — literally "stiff corpse" — is the Chinese hopping vampire, and the first thing to understand about it is that the "vampire" label is a Western convenience. The jiangshi does not, in the older stories, drink blood. It drains *qi*, the vital life-force, from the living, leaving victims drained of colour and warmth. It is stiff because it is a corpse in rigor: it cannot bend its limbs, so it moves by hopping, arms held out in front, and it is blind, finding the living by their breath. Hold your breath and stay still, the stories say, and it may hop straight past you.

The jiangshi's look — Qing-dynasty official's robes, a tall round-topped hat, and a yellow paper talisman (*fulu*) pasted to its forehead — is largely the creation of Hong Kong cinema, especially the beloved *Mr. Vampire* films of the 1980s, which codified what had been a looser collection of corpse stories. But the roots are older: Qing scholars like Ji Xiaolan recorded corpse tales in the eighteenth century, and the tradition of "driving corpses" — Taoist practitioners transporting the dead home over long distances, the bodies bobbing upright on bamboo poles — gave the hopping gait a real-world shadow. The talisman is the key to the whole figure: pasted on, it holds the corpse still or under control; torn off, it sets it loose.

- **Variants:** the controlled corpse, transported home under a Taoist's talisman; the wild jiangshi, free and draining qi; the cinematic jiangshi of Hong Kong film, in mandarin robes with a fluttering *fulu*.
- **Defining traits:** stiffness and hopping; arms outstretched; blindness compensated by breath-sense; the forehead talisman; and a drain of life-force rather than blood.

### Vetala

*Overlap note: also tagged South Asian in the tracker; written here, once.*

The vetala is a spirit of Hindu tradition that haunts charnel grounds and cemeteries and takes possession of corpses, using dead bodies as vehicles it can enter and leave at will. It is not the corpse, and it is not quite a ghost of the person who died; it is a knowledgeable, often malicious spirit caught between life and afterlife, credited with fortune-telling, with driving people mad, and with harming children — and, in some accounts, with guarding villages. The English word "vampire" is sometimes used for it, but that flattens a stranger and more interesting figure: a vetala is a talker, a riddler, a spirit with opinions.

Its great story is the *Vetala Panchavimshati*, the Twenty-Five Tales of the Vetala, preserved in Somadeva's eleventh-century *Kathasaritsagara* and in other recensions. A sorcerer sends King Vikramaditya to a cremation ground to fetch a corpse hanging from a tree — the corpse is possessed by a vetala — and to carry it back without speaking. The vetala tells the king a story on the way, ends it with a riddle, and warns him that if he knows the answer and stays silent, his head will burst; if he answers, the vetala will fly back to its tree. Vikramaditya answers, twenty-five times, and the vetala returns, twenty-five times. The frame is a contest of wits, not of strength, and the vetala wins it by being more interesting than the king's errand.

- **Variants:** the charnel-ground spirit of Hindu folklore; the vetala of the Vikramaditya tales, a riddling storyteller; Betal or Vetal of the Konkan region, where the spirit is also worshipped and tied to the goddess Shantadurga.
- **Defining traits:** possession of corpses rather than identity with them; a love of riddles and stories; knowledge of hidden things; and a home among the dead that it treats as a vantage point, not a prison.

### Revenant

"Revenant" — from the French *revenir*, to come back — is the umbrella word for the dead who return in bodily form, and it covers more ground than any single monster. Medieval chronicles are full of them: William of Newburgh in twelfth-century England records corpses that rose, wandered, and spread disease until they were dug up, staked, or burned; Scandinavian sagas give us the draugr; Slavic tradition gives us the vampire. The revenant, in these accounts, is not a spirit. It has weight, it leaves footprints, it can be fought with ordinary weapons, and it is usually driven by a narrow purpose — revenge on a murderer, a wrong to right, a debt to collect.

That narrowness is what separates the revenant from the zombie and the ghost. A ghost haunts; a zombie hungers; a revenant has a target. Modern fiction has kept the word for exactly that figure: the dead man who walks back for one person, one deed, and will not be distracted from it. The horror is not that the dead walk, but that they walk with intent — that death did not end the argument, it only paused it.

- **Variants:** the medieval chronicle revenant, spreading disease and terror; the vengeance revenant of folklore and fiction, fixed on one target; the saga draugr, its Norse cousin.
- **Defining traits:** a physical, returning body; a single fixed purpose, usually revenge; indifference to everything that is not that purpose; and a persistence that ends only when the purpose is fulfilled or the body is destroyed.

### Sluagh

*Overlap note: also tagged Celtic & British Isles in the tracker; written here, once.*

The Sluagh — *Sluagh na marbh*, the Host of the Dead — are the unforgiven dead of Irish and Scottish folklore, flying through the night in a great host. The word itself simply means "host, army, crowd," from Old Irish *slúag*, and it survives in English inside "slogan," from *sluagh-ghairm*, the battle-cry of the host. In the Western Isles, the Sluagh were described as the souls of the dead flying through the air, especially at Hallowe'en, the feast of the dead and the festival of the fairies. They take a crescent form, like a flight of birds — starlings, in the accounts collected by Alexander Carmichael and John Gregorson Campbell — and they can approach from any direction, seize a person, and carry them far away through the air.

The Sluagh are the dead who were too wicked, or too unrepentant, to be received anywhere else — not heaven, not hell, not rest. So they fly, endlessly, bitter and envious of the warm houses below, and they gather around the dying to snatch souls, and around houses where someone lies ill — which is why, in the Highlands and Islands, west-facing windows and doors were closed when someone was on their deathbed, because the Host was said to come from the west, the direction of the setting sun and of death. Some accounts make them fallen angels; others place them among the Unseelie Court, the dark fairies. Either way, they are a crowd, not a person, and that is their terror: you cannot bargain with a flock.

- **Variants:** the Irish and Scottish Host of the Dead; the Hebridean bird-flock, starlings or dark crows; the Sluagh as fallen angels or as the Unseelie host.
- **Defining traits:** flight in a crescent host; a western approach; soul-snatching around the dying; and a restlessness that is punishment, not choice.

### Wight Lord

*Thin lore — director call.* There is no "Wight Lord" in folklore as a named figure. There are wights — Old English *wiht*, a being, a creature — and there are barrow wights and mound-dwellers, and there are lords among the dead in the sense that a buried king or chieftain keeps his rank in the grave: the draugr of a chieftain guards his hoard, and the barrow of a king is a more dangerous place than the barrow of a commoner. But a distinct creature called a Wight Lord, commanding lesser undead, is a fantasy composite, built from the barrow wight, the draugr chieftain, and the "death knight" or "wight king" of tabletop and video games.

What folklore does offer is the principle behind the composite: the dead keep their social order. A king's mound is guarded more fiercely than a peasant's; a war leader's dead still follow him; and the most dangerous dead man in a saga is usually the one who was most powerful in life. The Wight Lord entry should be read as that principle given a crown — a ruler of the lesser dead because he ruled the living — not as a figure with its own stories, and the director should decide how much of the fantasy composite to keep.

- **Variants:** none in folklore; the closest kin are the draugr chieftain guarding his hoard and the buried king of barrow lore.
- **Defining traits:** command over lesser dead; a crown or kingly remnant; and the barrow wight's territorial ferocity, scaled up to a ruler's.

### Lich

The lich is, unusually for this bestiary, almost entirely a modern literary invention — and it is honest to say so. The word "lich" is Old English *līc*, simply a body or corpse, and it survives in "lychgate," the churchyard gate, and "lichway," a corpse road. But the lich as a powerful undead sorcerer who has hidden his soul away from his body comes from twentieth-century fantasy, above all from *Dungeons & Dragons*, where the lich keeps its soul in a phylactery — a vessel whose destruction is the only way to kill it for good. The phylactery, the soul-jar, and the lich's whole apparatus of undeath-by-choice are game and novel inventions, not folk beliefs.

The older material the lich draws on is real enough: the sorcerer who will not die, the wizard who bargains with death, the dead king who rules from his tomb, and the widespread folk idea that a person's life can be hidden in an object — an egg, a needle, a chest — so that they cannot be killed while the object survives. That last idea is genuinely old and widespread, from Slavic tales of a deathless sorcerer whose death is hidden in a needle in an egg in a duck in a hare in a chest, to countless fairy tales of giants and witches with external hearts. The lich gathers those threads into one figure: death not as an accident but as a project, pursued deliberately by someone powerful enough to treat mortality as a problem to be solved.

- **Variants:** the *Dungeons & Dragons* lich, soul in a phylactery; the Slavic deathless sorcerer, the folk original of the hidden-death idea; the sorcerer-king who rules from his tomb in pulp and fantasy fiction.
- **Defining traits:** undeath by choice and by magic; a soul hidden outside the body; great magical power kept past death; and a cold, patient intelligence that no shambling corpse possesses.

### Dullahan

*Overlap note: also tagged Celtic & British Isles in the tracker; written here, once.*

The Dullahan — Irish *dúlachán*, sometimes *dubhlachan* — is the headless rider of Irish folklore, a harbinger of death who rides a black horse through the night carrying his own head under his arm or in his hand. Wherever he stops, someone nearby dies; when he calls out a name, the person named dies at once. Some accounts give him a coach instead of a horse: the *cóiste bodhar*, the deaf or silent coach, built of coffin wood, its axles of human spine and its wheels of thigh bone, lit by candles in hollowed skulls, and accompanied, in some tellings, by a banshee. His whip, in the most repeated modern accounts, is made from a human spine, and he lashes it at anyone foolish enough to watch him pass, striking at their eyes.

The Dullahan's origins are tangled. Many folklorists link him to Crom Dubh, a dark harvest figure of pre-Christian Ireland to whom human sacrifice was said to be offered, and to older Celtic beliefs about the head as the seat of the soul. It is also worth being honest about the record: the Dullahan is thinly attested in native Irish sources — he does not appear in the Irish Folklore Commission's collections in the way better-documented figures do — and the fullest early written accounts come from Thomas Crofton Croker's *Fairy Legends and Traditions of the South of Ireland* (1828), which preserved and may have polished the tales. The headless horseman is a wider European and American archetype, from Washington Irving's Sleepy Hollow onward, but the Dullahan is the Irish form: not a ghost seeking revenge, but death's own courier, doing a job that cannot be refused.

- **Variants:** the mounted headless rider; the coachman of the *cóiste bodhar*; the female headless rider in some of Croker's tales.
- **Defining traits:** the carried head, with eyes that see everywhere; the black horse or death coach; the spine whip; and a single spoken name as a death sentence.

*Abysmal take: Abysmal gives the Dullahan a thrown-head strike; in folklore the head is carried and its gaze and spoken name are the doom, not a thrown weapon.*

### Gashadokuro

*Overlap note: also tagged Japanese Yōkai in the tracker; written here, once.*

The Gashadokuro — "rattling skeleton," from the clattering sound *gasha-gasha* and *dokuro*, skull — is a giant skeleton made from the bones of the dead who were never properly buried: soldiers left on battlefields, victims of famine, people who died alone and were forgotten. Their bones gather, animated by resentment, into a single enormous figure, ten metres tall or more, that wanders the night after midnight, snatches lone travellers, bites off their heads, and drinks their blood. Only its eyes are said to stand out, burning yellow or green in the dark, and its approach is announced by the rattling of its own bones.

Honesty about the Gashadokuro's age matters, because the creature looks ancient and is not. The named Gashadokuro first appeared in print in the mid-twentieth century — a description in 1966, then Shigeru Mizuki's illustrations and the yōkai encyclopaedias of the late 1960s and 1970s. Its famous image borrows from an older work that was never about it: Utagawa Kuniyoshi's circa-1844 triptych *Takiyasha the Witch and the Skeleton Spectre*, in which the sorceress Takiyasha, daughter of the rebel Taira no Masakado, summons a giant skeleton at the ruined palace of Sōma. Later writers tied the two together, and even invented a tenth-century origin involving Takiyasha conjuring the first Gashadokuro from battle dead. The real story is more interesting: a modern yōkai built from a real social fear — that a society which leaves its dead unburied will be haunted by them, all at once, at giant scale.

- **Variants:** the *ōdokuro*, the great skull, a near-synonym; the Kuniyoshi skeleton spectre, the visual ancestor but not the same creature; the *hidarugami*, the separate spirit said to form when the bones' original souls depart.
- **Defining traits:** colossal scale; a body of many dead, not one; night wandering after midnight; bone-rattle as a warning; and a hunger that is really a grudge — the anger of the unburied, given a body.

### Penanggalan

The penanggalan — from Malay *tanggal*, to detach or take off — is a nocturnal vampiric being of Malay folklore that takes the form of a woman's floating head, its organs and entrails trailing from the neck. By day she is an ordinary woman, often said to be a midwife or healer; by night her head detaches and flies, hunting above all for pregnant women and newborn children, feeding with a long tongue, or reaching through floorboards with her trailing entrails. She smells of vinegar, because she must soak her organs in vinegar to shrink them enough to fit back into her body before dawn — a detail so specific and so repeated that it functions in the stories as a test: a woman who smells of vinegar, who avoids being seen at night, may be a penanggalan.

The penanggalan is, strictly speaking, not a corpse. She is a living woman who has learned, through black magic and a ritual bath in vinegar, to take this form — or, in one famous origin tale, a woman startled during her ritual bath who jerked her head up so violently that it tore free, organs and all, and who flew off in rage after the man who startled her. The creature belongs to a whole Southeast Asian family of flying-head beings: the Thai *krasue*, the Cambodian *ahp*, the Lao *kasu*, the Filipino *manananggal* (which severs at the waist and grows wings), and the Balinese *leyak*. All of them circle the same real-world fear — the dangers of childbirth in a world without modern medicine — and all of them make the woman who should help at a birth into the thing that threatens it.

- **Variants:** the Malay penanggalan; the Thai *krasue*; the Cambodian *ahp*; the Lao *kasu*; the Filipino *manananggal*; the Balinese *leyak*.
- **Defining traits:** a detached head with trailing viscera; a daytime life as an ordinary woman; a hunger for birth blood and newborns; and the smell of vinegar as a giveaway.

*Abysmal take: Abysmal files the Penanggalan with the Undead for bestiary purposes; in folklore she is a living witch in a detachable form, not a risen corpse.*

## Celestials & the Fallen

Heaven, in the traditions these creatures come from, is not a soft place. It is a court, an army, and a throne room, and its inhabitants are described as terrifying before they are described as kind. The angels of the Hebrew Bible and the devas of Hindu and Buddhist tradition are messengers and warriors and living wheels of fire, and the fallen among them are terrifying for a different reason: they remember exactly what they were, and they have chosen otherwise.

### Deva

In Hindu and Buddhist tradition, a deva is a shining one — a god, a celestial being, a dweller in a higher realm who enjoys the rewards of good karma but is not, in the Buddhist telling, free of the cycle of rebirth. Devas are radiant, long-lived, and powerful, and they are also, in Buddhist thought, in a spiritually dangerous position: a life of pleasure in a heaven realm can make liberation seem unnecessary, and when a deva's merit runs out, they fall back into lower rebirths like anyone else. In Hindu tradition, the devas are the gods of the Vedic pantheon and its successors — Indra, Agni, Surya, and their host — beings of order who uphold the cosmos and who war, in the old stories, against the asuras, their rivals and kin.

A deva in the stories is therefore not a generic "good angel." It is a being of a particular rank in a particular cosmos, with duties, rivalries, and limits. Devas can be proud, can be tricked, can lose battles, and can need the help of humans and heroes. Their radiance is not a moral judgement; it is what they are made of, the way fire is made of heat. To meet a deva in a story is usually to meet someone who outranks you and knows it, and who may help you or may simply be passing through on business of their own.

- **Variants:** the Vedic devas, gods of sky, fire, and storm; the Buddhist devas of the desire and form realms, radiant but still bound to rebirth; the guardian devas of later Hindu and Buddhist art.
- **Defining traits:** radiance; long life without immortality; a place in a ranked heaven; and power that is real but not absolute.

### Seraph

The seraphim come from a single, unforgettable passage: Isaiah's vision of the throne of God, where seraphim stand above the throne, each with six wings — two covering the face, two covering the feet, and two for flying — crying "Holy, holy, holy" without ceasing. The name comes from a Hebrew root meaning to burn, and the seraphim are burning ones, creatures of fire and praise so intense that even they must veil themselves before the throne they serve. One of them touches Isaiah's lips with a burning coal and purges his sin, which is the seraph's other great image: fire that cleanses rather than merely destroys.

Later tradition, especially the Christian angelology of Pseudo-Dionysius, placed the seraphim at the very top of the angelic hierarchy, closest to God, defined by love and by burning. They are not, in the Bible, warriors or messengers in the ordinary sense; they are worshippers, and their worship is their work. That is what makes them frightening in art and story: a being whose entire existence is praise, whose fire never goes out, and who looks at you, if it looks at you at all, from behind wings held over its own face.

- **Variants:** the seraphim of Isaiah's throne vision; the highest choir of Pseudo-Dionysian angelology; the burning, six-winged figure of Christian art.
- **Defining traits:** six wings; fire; unceasing praise; and a holiness so intense it must be veiled even from itself.

### Ophanim

The ophanim — the "wheels" — come from Ezekiel's vision of the divine chariot, the Merkabah. Ezekiel sees, beside each of the four living creatures, a wheel within a wheel, rims covered with eyes, moving in any direction without turning, lifted by the same spirit that lifts the creatures, gleaming like topaz or beryl. They are not angels with faces in the ordinary sense; they are the machinery of the throne, the wheels of a chariot that goes wherever the spirit goes, and their many eyes mean they see everywhere at once. Later Jewish mysticism and Christian angelology made the ophanim — also called thrones — a distinct order of angels, defined by submission and by carrying the divine presence.

The ophanim's strangeness is the point. Ezekiel's vision is deliberately hard to picture — wheels inside wheels, eyes on rims, movement without turning — because it is trying to describe something that does not fit ordinary categories of body and motion. In art they are usually shown as rings of eyes and wings, or as great golden wheels, and they are among the most alien figures in the whole angelic tradition: not humanoid at all, but a geometry that watches you.

- **Variants:** the wheels of Ezekiel's Merkabah vision; the thrones of later angelology; the eye-covered wheels of mystical art.
- **Defining traits:** a wheel-within-a-wheel form; rims full of eyes; movement in any direction without turning; and sight that has no blind side.

*Abysmal take: Abysmal gives the Ophanim sweeping eye-beams as an attack; in Ezekiel the eyes are for seeing, not for striking, and the wheels are the throne's bearers, not its weapons.*

### Watcher (Grigori)

The Watchers — the Grigori, from the Greek for "wakeful ones" — come from the Book of Enoch, an ancient Jewish text that did not make it into most biblical canons but shaped angel lore for centuries. In Enoch, the Watchers are angels sent to watch over humanity who instead descend to Mount Hermon, take human wives, and teach forbidden arts: weapons, cosmetics, sorcery, astrology. Their children are the giants, the Nephilim, and the corruption they bring is one reason given for the Flood. The leaders of the fallen Watchers, Samyaza and Azazel, are bound in darkness until judgement, and Azazel in particular becomes a figure of blame — the scapegoat sent into the wilderness on the Day of Atonement carries his name.

The Watcher is therefore a figure of failed guardianship: a being whose job was to watch and not touch, who touched, and whose touch ruined both worlds. In later tradition, "watcher" kept both senses — the guardian who sees all, and the fallen one who fell by looking too closely at what he guarded. That double sense is what makes the Watcher a good monster: it is not ignorant of you, and it is not indifferent. It has been watching you for a long time, and watching, in its story, is how the trouble started.

- **Variants:** the Enochic Watchers of Mount Hermon; Azazel, the bound teacher of forbidden arts; Samyaza, their leader; the later "watcher" as guardian angel, fallen or unfallen.
- **Defining traits:** a watching post over humanity; forbidden knowledge taught to mortals; giant offspring; and a fall that comes from attention, not rebellion alone.

### Fallen Angel

The fallen angel is the great hinge of Christian and Jewish demonology: an angel who was once in heaven and is no longer. The story is told in several forms that later tradition fused into one. Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon — "How you have fallen from heaven, morning star, son of the dawn" — was read as the fall of Lucifer. The Book of Revelation's war in heaven, in which Michael and his angels fight the dragon, ends with the dragon and his angels thrown down to earth. The Enochic Watchers (see above) fall by descent and desire rather than by war. Out of these threads came the figure who dominates Western demonology: Lucifer, the light-bearer, brightest of angels, whose pride made him refuse to serve, and who took a third of the host with him.

A fallen angel, in the stories, is not a different species from an angel. It is an angel with its allegiance changed and its light dimmed but not extinguished. That is why fallen angels in literature — from Milton's Satan, who would rather reign in hell than serve in heaven, to the brooding fallen angels of modern fantasy — are so often intelligent, articulate, and proud rather than bestial. They remember what they were, they can argue theology with you, and their cruelty has the shape of a choice rather than an instinct. They fell; they were not made to fall.

- **Variants:** Lucifer, the morning star of Isaiah and Milton; the dragon's angels of Revelation; the fallen Watchers of Enoch; the many named fallen angels of later demonology, from Azazel to Belial.
- **Defining traits:** former radiance kept in dimmed form; intelligence and pride; a fall by choice, whether pride, desire, or rebellion; and a knowledge of heaven that makes their opposition personal.

### Ashen Seraph

*Thin lore — director call.* There is no "Ashen Seraph" in scripture, angelology, or folklore as a named being. There are seraphim (see above), there are fallen angels, and there are angels of fire and judgement whose wings could be imagined as scorched — but an ashen seraph, a burnt seraph, is a modern composite, a seraph after a fire it did not survive intact. The behaviour specs already flag this entry as thin, and that flag is correct: the name is an Abysmal invention built from two real ingredients, the seraph and the idea of a fallen or burnt angel, without a tradition of its own.

What can honestly be said is what the composite suggests: a seraph is a burning one, so an ashen seraph is a burning one whose fire has gone out or gone wrong — praise turned to cinders, six wings ragged, light replaced by smoke. That is a vivid image, and it fits the family of fallen celestials, but it should be presented as the game's own figure, not as a creature from any scripture or folk tradition, and the director should decide whether the name stays or whether the creature is better presented as a fallen seraph under a different title.

- **Variants:** none in tradition; the closest kin are the fallen seraphim of apocryphal and literary angelology and the burnt, exiled angels of modern fantasy.
- **Defining traits:** six wings, ragged or ashen; fire turned to ember and smoke; and the seraph's discipline kept after its light has failed.

## Demons, Devils & the Damned

Demons are older than devils, and the distinction matters. A demon, in the ancient world, could be a spirit of any moral colour — the Greek *daimon* was a guiding spirit, not necessarily evil. A devil is a demon with a job in a moral cosmos: a tempter, an accuser, a bargainer, a jailer of the damned. This family gathers both, and the damned who serve them, because in the stories they keep the same company: the pit, the crossroads, the bargain, and the soul as a thing that can be owed.

### Imp

The imp is the small devil of European folklore and early modern demonology — a minor demon, a familiar, a mischief-maker rather than a prince of hell. Imps belong to witches as familiars in the witch-trial literature of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, where they suckled from "witch's marks" and did small, spiteful errands, and they belong to church carvings and manuscript margins as the little horned figures who pull faces at the saints. The Lincoln Imp, carved in Lincoln Cathedral and explained by a legend in which the devil's imps were turned to stone for misbehaving in church, is the most famous English example: an imp is a devil small enough to be a nuisance, and the stories treat it accordingly, with exasperation rather than terror.

Later fantasy kept the imp small and made it a servant — of wizards, of greater demons, of hell's bureaucracy — and gave it fire, because small devils in art so often carry flames or sit in them. The imp's defining quality across all of this is scale: it is not powerful enough to damn you, only to annoy, mislead, and set small fires, literal and otherwise. A single imp is a pest. A swarm of them is a problem, because imps, like all small devils, work best in numbers and in the corners of a room where a larger devil would not fit.

- **Variants:** the witch's familiar imp of trial literature; the Lincoln Imp of cathedral legend; the fantasy imp, a small fire-devil in a greater demon's service.
- **Defining traits:** small size; horns and a tail in most depictions; mischief over malice; fire; and a tendency to appear in groups or in someone else's service.

### Possessed Cultist

*Thin lore — director call.* The possessed cultist is not a creature of folklore. It is a figure of modern horror — the human whose body has been taken over by something else, acting in a cult's service — and its parts come from several different traditions that should not be blurred together. Demonic possession is real in the traditions that describe it, from the Gospels' possessed men to the exorcism literature of the Catholic Church and the spirit-possession practices of many cultures, in which a spirit enters a person and speaks or acts through them. Cults, in the older sense of a religious group outside the mainstream, are a different thing again, and the "cultist" as a robed villain in a dungeon is largely a pulp and gaming invention.

What the composite gets right is the horror of the combination: a person who is still visibly a person, moving wrong, speaking with a voice that is not quite theirs, serving a purpose they did not choose. Possession stories agree on the signs — a changed voice, unnatural strength, knowledge the person should not have, aversion to holy things — and they agree that the person inside is, in the telling, a victim as much as a threat. That victimhood is worth keeping in any telling: a possessed cultist is a person first, and the thing wearing them second, and the tragedy is that you cannot always tell where one ends and the other begins.

- **Variants:** none as a single folklore figure; the closest kin are the possessed person of exorcism literature, the medium or horse of spirit-possession traditions, and the cult devotee of pulp horror.
- **Defining traits:** a human body moved by another will; jerky or wrong movement; a voice that does not belong; and service to a power the person did not choose.

### Succubus

The succubus — from Latin *succuba*, "one who lies beneath" — is a female demon of medieval Christian demonology who visits men in their sleep, in dreams, to seduce them and, in the theology of the time, to steal their seed. The male counterpart is the incubus (see below). The great medieval systematiser of the idea was the scholastic tradition that asked, seriously, how demons could have children: the answer, repeated from Augustine onward, was that a succubus first took seed from a man, then, changing form into an incubus, delivered it to a woman — a theory that produced, among other things, the legend that Merlin was the son of an incubus. The succubus is therefore not only a seducer but a thief, and her theft was treated as a real spiritual danger, not a metaphor.

Older roots run deeper than the medieval demonologists. The night-visitant who lies on sleepers and drains them appears in many cultures — the Mesopotamian Lilitu, the night demon Lilith of Jewish tradition, the Slavic mora and the Germanic mare who sit on sleepers' chests and give them nightmares. The succubus gathers those figures into a Christian demonology of temptation: she comes at night, when will is weakest, and her danger is not force but consent — the victim, in the stories, wants her there, and that wanting is the trap.

- **Variants:** the medieval succubus of demonology; Lilith, the night demon of Jewish tradition; the *mora* and *mare*, the nightmare-sitters of Slavic and Germanic lore.
- **Defining traits:** night visitation; seduction in dreams; a draining of vitality, seed, or life-force; and a danger that works through desire rather than violence.

### Incubus

The incubus — from Latin *incubo*, "one who lies upon" — is the male counterpart of the succubus, a demon who visits women in their sleep to lie with them. In medieval demonology the two are a pair, and often literally the same demon in two forms, shifting sex to carry seed from one victim to another, as in the Merlin legend. But the incubus also has a life outside that pairing: in Roman tradition, the incubus was a household spirit, and in later European folklore the incubus shades into the nightmare itself — the weight on the chest, the paralysis of sleep, the sense of a presence in a dark room that will not let you move or cry out.

That overlap with sleep paralysis is the incubus's most durable trait. Across cultures, people waking unable to move have described a figure pressing on them, and the stories gave that figure names: incubus, mare, mora, *karabasan*, Old Hag. The incubus, in the demonological telling, turns that helplessness erotic and predatory: he does not merely sit on you, he takes from you, and he returns, because the visit is the point. Modern retellings often make him a seducer rather than a rapist, and that softening should be noted honestly: the older stories are about violation in sleep, not romance, and a bestiary that uses the name should not pretend otherwise.

- **Variants:** the medieval incubus of demonology; the Roman household incubus; the nightmare-presser of sleep-paralysis lore across cultures; the father of Merlin in Arthurian legend.
- **Defining traits:** night visitation; a weight or embrace that holds the sleeper; a draining or taking in sleep; and a return to the same victim.

### Pit Brute

*Thin lore — director call.* There is no "Pit Brute" in folklore, demonology, or scripture as a named creature. The name is a descriptive composite — a brute from a pit — and the behaviour specs already flag it as thin. The closest traditional material is general rather than specific: the demons of the pit in Christian art and in Dante's *Inferno*, where the Malebranche and other demons torment the damned with hooks and pitchforks; the giants and ogres who guard the underworld in many traditions; and the "brute" demon of later fantasy, a large, stupid, violent devil whose job is to stand in the way and hit things.

What the composite draws on is real enough — hell, in the Christian imagination, has always had labourers as well as princes, and the pit has always needed someone to do the heavy work — but no single story, name, or tradition attaches to a creature called a Pit Brute. The entry should be read as a job description for a kind of demon rather than as a figure from lore, and the director should decide whether the name is kept as a game-original or replaced with a named demon of the pit tradition.

- **Variants:** none as a named figure; the closest kin are Dante's Malebranche, the tormenting demons of the pit, and the ogre-guards of underworld gates.
- **Defining traits:** great size and strength; a pit or hellish home; a role as guard, labourer, or enforcer; and violence without cunning.

### Djinn

The djinn — singular *jinni*, from Arabic *jinn*, "hidden" — are, in Islamic tradition, a whole people, not a single monster. Made of smokeless fire where humans are made of clay and angels of light, the jinn live alongside humanity, unseen, with their own societies, religions, and kings. The Qur'an speaks of them directly, and of Iblis, the jinn who refused to bow to Adam and became the tempter. Some jinn are Muslim, some are not; some are helpful, most are indifferent, and some are actively hostile. They are not demons in the Christian sense, and they are not gods. They are neighbours, in a world that has more neighbours than the eye can see.

The wish-granting djinn of Western story — the genie in the lamp or the bottle — comes from the *One Thousand and One Nights*, especially the tale of Aladdin, where the jinni of the lamp and the jinni of the ring are servants bound to objects, and their power is vast but tied to a master's commands. Western retellings kept the wishes and dropped almost everything else: the jinn's societies, their religions, their free will, and the fact that in Islamic tradition a jinni is a person, not a vending machine. The stories that take jinn seriously are usually about the danger of dealing with a free, intelligent, invisible neighbour who does not share your interests.

- **Variants:** the Qur'anic jinn, a people of smokeless fire; the *ghūl* and other hostile kinds (see Ghoul); the bound jinni of the lamp and the ring in the *Nights*; the *ifrit*, *marid*, and *shaitan* below, which are kinds or ranks of jinn.
- **Defining traits:** invisibility; fire as their element; free will and societies of their own; great power bound, in the famous tales, to a lamp, ring, or bottle.

*Abysmal take: Abysmal specs no wish-granting in combat; in the tales, wishes belong to bargaining with a bound jinni, not to a fight.*

### Larva of the Damned

*Thin lore — director call.* The name is a composite, and the behaviour specs already flag it as thin, but the pieces it is built from are ancient. In Roman belief, the *larvae* — also called *lemures* — were restless, malignant spirits of the dead, the shades of people who died violently, prematurely, or without proper burial, who haunted the living and had to be driven out. The Roman festival of Lemuria, in May, was a household rite for expelling them: the head of the household walked barefoot through the house at midnight, threw black beans over his shoulder, and told the spirits to leave, nine times. The *larvae* were not worms and not a single monster; they were the unquiet dead as a class, feared precisely because they had no single face.

Later tradition kept the word but changed the shape. In medieval and early modern demonology, larvae became minor demons or tormented souls. In modern fantasy, most famously in *Dungeons & Dragons*, the larva became a worm-like soul of the damned crawling in the lower planes, a soul reduced to its lowest form. Abysmal's "Larva of the Damned" is closest to that modern shape — a mass of damned souls, writhing, many-mouthed — but the name reaches back to Rome, and the entry should keep both halves visible: the Roman restless dead, and the modern fantasy worm, with the composite acknowledged as the game's own.

- **Variants:** the Roman *larvae* or *lemures*, restless shades expelled at Lemuria; the medieval larva as minor demon; the modern fantasy larva, a worm-soul of the damned.
- **Defining traits:** restlessness after a bad death; a form that is many or shapeless rather than one person; and a hunger or hostility toward the living that has no single motive.

### Ifrit

The ifrit — Arabic *ʿifrīt*, plural *ʿafārīt* — is a powerful and often malevolent kind of jinni, named in the Qur'an itself, where an ifrit of the jinn offers to bring Solomon the Queen of Sheba's throne before he rises from his seat. In later folklore and in the *One Thousand and One Nights*, ifrits are among the strongest of the jinn: huge, winged, made of fire and smoke, proud, dangerous, and not to be trifled with. They are associated with ruins, with the underworld, and with the dead, and in some traditions the ifrit is specifically the spirit of a murdered person seeking vengeance — a ghost with a jinni's power.

Ifrits are not a single character but a rank. Where a common jinni might be bargained with, an ifrit must be commanded, bound, or avoided, and the stories about them are usually about the cost of trying. They appear in the *Nights* as adversaries of heroes, as kings of their own tribes, and as beings whose oaths are binding but whose tempers are not. Their fire is not a metaphor: an ifrit is, in the tradition, a creature of heat and smoke, and its presence is described as a burning wind before it is described as a face.

- **Variants:** the Qur'anic ifrit at Solomon's court; the ifrit of the *Nights*, a winged king of the jinn; the vengeful ifrit of folk tradition, a murdered soul grown powerful.
- **Defining traits:** great power among the jinn; fire and smoke; wings in many depictions; pride; and a danger that scales with the ambition of whoever summons them.

### Marid

The marid — Arabic *mārid*, "rebellious" — is, in the traditional ranking of the jinn, the most powerful and the most proud of all, above even the ifrit. In the *One Thousand and One Nights* and in later folklore, marids are the great ones: vast, ancient, associated with the sea and with open water, and so mighty that only the greatest magicians or prophets can bind them. The fisherman's tale in the *Nights*, in which a poor fisherman releases a jinni from a bottle and must outwit it to survive, is often told of a marid, and the point of the tale is the scale of the power involved: a marid's gratitude is a fortune, and a marid's anger is a disaster.

The marid's association with water is less fixed than some modern retellings suggest — marids are primarily great and rebellious, and water is one of their homes rather than their definition — but it is a persistent one, and it fits their character: deep, old, patient, and indifferent to human timescales. A marid in a story is rarely a servant. It is a power that has been, for a time, inconvenienced by a bottle, and it remembers the inconvenience.

- **Variants:** the marid of the *Nights*, the mightiest jinni; the sea-marid of later folklore; the marid as a rebellious king among the jinn.
- **Defining traits:** supreme power among the jinn; pride and rebellion as a defining character; an association with the sea and deep water; and a scale that makes bargaining with one a gamble, not a transaction.

### Contract Devil

*Thin lore — director call.* There is no single folklore creature called a "Contract Devil," but the devil who bargains is one of the most durable figures in Western tradition, and the entry is best read as that tradition under a descriptive name. The pattern is old: a human in need — wealth, knowledge, love, life — meets a devil who offers it, for a price, and the price is always the soul, or something the human will regret more. The *Faust* legend, from the sixteenth-century chapbook through Marlowe's *Doctor Faustus* to Goethe's *Faust*, is the great telling: Faustus signs away his soul for twenty-four years of power, served by Mephistopheles, a devil who is polite, learned, and absolutely literal about the contract. The contract's literalness is the horror: the devil keeps his word exactly, and the human discovers that exactly was not what they meant.

Other tellings sharpen different edges. In folk tales, the devil is often outwitted — by a trick, a loophole, or a saint's intervention — because the bargain, like any bargain, has terms, and terms can be read closely. In Christian theology, the devil cannot compel; he can only tempt, and the bargain is the human's own choice made formal. The "contract devil" as a named monster is a gaming and modern-fiction compression of all of this: the tempter with paperwork, the negotiator who knows he has time. It is a useful figure, but it is a type, not a named being, and the director should keep that distinction in view.

- **Variants:** Mephistopheles, Faustus's devil; the folk devil outwitted at the crossroads or the bridge; the crossroads demon of American blues legend, who trades skill for a soul.
- **Defining traits:** a bargain offered in plain terms; a price that is always higher than it looks; literalness about wording; and patience — the devil, in these stories, can always wait.

### Shaitan

The shaitan — Arabic *shayṭān*, plural *shayāṭīn* — is, in Islamic tradition, a devil, a rebel among the jinn who tempts humans away from God. The great shaitan is Iblis, also called al-Shaytan, the jinni who refused God's command to bow to Adam and was cast out, becoming the tempter of humanity. But *shaitan* is also a general word: any rebellious jinni, any tempter, any spirit that whispers evil can be called a shaitan, and the Qur'an speaks of shayatin among both jinn and humans. The word entered English as "Satan" through Hebrew and Greek, but in Arabic it kept its wider sense — not one devil, but a kind.

Shaitans in folklore are therefore less a monster with a shape than a role: the whisperer, the inciter, the one who makes a bad choice look good. They are associated with deserts, ruins, and lonely places, like the ghoul, and with storms and wildness in some traditions. In later fantasy, "shaitan" was borrowed as a name for a great earth or storm devil — a rank among the jinn, alongside ifrit and marid — but that ranking is a modern systematisation, not a Qur'anic hierarchy. The older and more honest picture is simpler and more unsettling: a shaitan is any powerful, rebellious spirit that wants you to choose wrong, and it does not need a body to do its work.

- **Variants:** Iblis, the great shaitan; the shayatin as a class of rebellious jinn; the storm and desert shaitan of folk tradition; the modern fantasy shaitan, a ranked great devil.
- **Defining traits:** rebellion against God; temptation by whispering and suggestion; an association with waste places and storms; and a power that works through human choice rather than force.
